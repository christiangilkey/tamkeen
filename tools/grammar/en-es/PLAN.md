# English → Spanish (en-es): the plan

Spanish taught to an English speaker, explanations in English. Variety: **Mexican Spanish**, as the
basics course (`tools/course/LANGUAGES.md`: es-MX, *ustedes*, never *vosotros*).

## What makes this course Mexican
- *ustedes* for every "you all" (friends included); no *vosotros* forms anywhere.
- Mexican words where they differ: *carro*, *celular*, *computadora*, *camión* (city bus), *departamento*
  (apartment), *recámara*, *regadera* (shower), *playera*, *pluma* (pen), *jugo*, *papa*, *cuadra*,
  *manejar* (drive), *boleto*, *platicar*, *ahorita*, *¿Mande?*, *¡Qué padre!* (as a note). Never *coger*.
- Mexicans use the **preterite** for what happened today too (*¿Ya comiste?*), and the present perfect
  mostly for experiences and "so far" (*Nunca he ido a Oaxaca*). The course teaches it that way.
- The future is said with *ir a* + infinitive; the simple future (*será, tendrá*) is taught for
  predictions, promises and guesses (*¿Dónde estará?*), the way it is really used.
- Requests the Mexican way: *¿Me da…?*, *¿Me pasas…?*, *¿Me puede…?*, *Quisiera…*.
- Times of day *en la mañana / en la tarde / en la noche*.

## The order, and why
An English speaker can say something useful from lesson 1 with *soy* and *me llamo*; *ser* and *estar*
come in the first three lessons, side by side, because the choice between them is the first thing that
makes Spanish Spanish, and they come back in almost every unit. Gender and agreement come next (every
noun and adjective needs them). Then the present tense in three steps (-ar; -er/-ir; the irregular
"go"-verbs and the stem-changers), with the most useful irregulars (*querer, tener, ir, poder, hacer*)
early as chunks, before the patterns behind them. *Me gusta* comes early (lesson 11) because learners
want it at once and because its back-to-front shape prepares the object pronouns. *Ir a* + infinitive
gives a future right after *ir*. The past comes in unit 6: the preterite (events) first, then the
imperfect (background, habits), then the two together for telling a story. Commands come after the
past and build the "opposite vowel" forms (*hable, coma*) that the subjunctive then reuses at B1 for
wishes, advice, *cuando* and opinions. The conditional and *si* clauses close the course.

## The spiral: where each big structure comes back
- **ser / estar**: 1, 2, 3 → 5 (es vs está with adjectives) → 13 (está for where things are) → 15 (*ser*
  for when and where an event is) → 21 (*estar* + -ando) → 23 (*está nublado*) → 30 (*fue, estuvo*) →
  32–33 (*era, estaba*).
- **gender and agreement**: 2 → 4 → 5 → 10 (*mi/mis, nuestra*) → 12 (*este/esta*) → 26 (*lo/la*).
- **the present**: 7, 8 → 9 (questions) → 17–20 (irregulars, stem changes, reflexives) → 34 (*hace … que*
  + present) → 42 (*si* + present).
- **gustar and its pronouns**: 11 → 27 (*le/les* are the same pronouns) → 28 (*me duele, me encanta*) → 48
  (*me gustaría*).
- **ir**: 14 → 16 (*ir a* + infinitive) → 30 (*fui*) → 32 (*iba*) → 39 (*vaya*) → 41 (future vs *ir a*).
- **questions**: 1, 3 → 9 → 29–31 (*¿Qué hiciste?*) → 35 (*¿Alguna vez has…?*).
- **object pronouns**: 11 → 26, 27 → 35 (*nunca lo he probado*) → 38 (*dímelo*) → 40 (*no lo toques*).
- **the past**: 29 → 30, 31 → 32 → 33 (both together) → 34 → 35 → 50 (reporting).
- **the "opposite vowel" (subjunctive) forms**: 39 (usted commands) → 40 (negative commands) → 43–47 → 49.
- **tener**: 10 → 17 (*tener que*) → 23 (*tener calor*) → 30 (*tuve*).

## Conventions
- Every example is a whole Mexican Spanish sentence, accents and ¿ ¡ complete; English translations
  are natural English, not word-for-word (word-for-word goes in a note when it helps).
- `order` questions use sentences whose order is fixed; punctuation stays on the first and last words.
- `tests/js/grammar-es.test.mjs` checks the Spanish (accents on question words, ¿ ¡ pairs, no *vosotros*,
  common missing accents and tildes, words Mexico doesn't use) and the English (no British spellings).

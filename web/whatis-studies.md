# Supporting studies

The studies behind how Tamkeen is designed. Part 1 takes each design choice in turn: what Tamkeen does, what the research has found, and where the evidence is weak or mixed. Part 2 lists every study cited, with links.

## Part 1 · Why it is designed this way

Each design choice below says what Tamkeen does, what studies have found about it, and the studies themselves. Where the research is thin, mixed, or points the other way, it says so: some choices rest on strong evidence, and others are sensible guesses or simply there because they're fun.

### Reviews spaced out over time
**What Tamkeen does:** Tamkeen schedules every card with FSRS (the Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler, a memory model), aiming for a 90% chance that you still remember the word when it comes back. A word you know well comes back less and less often.
**What the research says:** Reviews spread out over time are remembered far better than the same number of reviews crammed together. This is one of the most reliable findings in memory research. A meta-analysis of 317 experiments found that spaced study beats massed study, and that the best gap between reviews grows the longer you need to remember (Cepeda et al., 2006). A meta-analysis of second-language studies found the same: spacing had a medium-to-large effect, and longer gaps beat shorter ones on delayed tests. In those studies, gaps that grow over time worked about as well as equal gaps (Kim & Webb, 2022). In a nine-year study of foreign-language vocabulary, 13 review sessions held 56 days apart gave about the same long-term memory as 26 sessions held 14 days apart (Bahrick et al., 1993; only four learners). FSRS builds on the work of Ye, Su and Cao (2022). They trained a memory model on 220 million review records from the MaiMemo language app and used it to schedule reviews at lower cost, reporting a 12.6% gain over earlier methods; the method was later extended (Su et al., 2023). Ebbinghaus (1885) first described the forgetting curve, and a 2015 single-learner replication produced a very similar curve. Limits: the FSRS papers measure how well the scheduler predicts memory and how few reviews it needs, using app data. They are not controlled tests of how much people learn. The 90% target is a trade-off between more reviews and more forgetting; no study fixes that number.

### Recall before you see the answer
**What Tamkeen does:** The front of a card shows the word and its sentence, and you try to remember the meaning before you turn it over. The answer is always shown after a miss.
**What the research says:** Trying to recall an answer strengthens memory more than reading it again. This is the "testing effect". In Roediger and Karpicke (2006), rereading did better on a test five minutes later, but practice testing gave much better memory days later. Karpicke and Roediger (2008) found the same with foreign-language word pairs: once a word had been answered correctly, testing it again greatly improved recall a week later, while studying it again did not. Students' predictions of how well they would do bore no relation to their actual results. A large meta-analysis confirmed the effect and found that recalling an answer helps more than recognising it from options (Rowland, 2014). Showing the correct answer after a miss matters a lot: in one word-learning study it increased final retention by 494%, while feedback after a correct answer made little difference (Pashler et al., 2005). Limits: most studies use university students in labs or classrooms, but the effect has been replicated many times, including for second-language words (Barcroft, 2007).

### Just two answer buttons
**What Tamkeen does:** You answer each card with Remembered or Forgot, instead of the four grades many flashcard apps use.
**What the research says:** There is no published study that compares two self-rating buttons with four for how much people learn. The evidence is indirect. Anki's official FSRS documentation says FSRS works fine if you only use "Again" and "Good", and "in some cases, FSRS may even be more accurate" that way. This is developer documentation, not a peer-reviewed study. Separately, research on self-grading shows that how honestly you grade yourself matters. Students who overrated their own recall while studying did worse on the final test, most likely because they stopped studying items they wrongly thought they knew (Dunlosky & Rawson, 2012). A simple, honest "did I get it?" is a reasonable design, but no study proves two buttons beat four.

### Which side of the card shows what
**What Tamkeen does:** The front shows the word in the language you're learning, with its sentence. The back shows the meaning and the sentence's translation in your own language. This trains understanding first: recognising a word when you hear or read it.
**What the research says:** The clearest finding is that you get best at the direction you practise. Steinel, Hulstijn and Steinel (2007) taught 20 English idioms to 129 Dutch students in one direction or the other. On a production test, learners who had practised producing the idiom (L1→L2) did much better. On a recognition test, learners who had practised recognising it (L2→L1) did better. Webb (2009) found that receptive learning (foreign word → meaning) produced larger gains in receptive knowledge of meaning. Productive learning (meaning → foreign word) produced larger gains in many other areas, including spelling and productive knowledge of meaning and grammar, so Webb suggested productive learning may be the better choice if only one is used. Griffin and Harley (1996) also found L1→L2 the more "versatile" order when learners need both to understand and to produce words. Recognising a word's meaning is the easiest kind of word knowledge and producing it from memory is the hardest. This order held at every frequency level in a test with 435 learners (Laufer & Goldstein, 2004). Producing words is harder still when they don't fit your own language's sound patterns (Ellis & Beaton, 1993). The harder L1→L2 direction can pay off: Schneider, Healy and Bourne (2002) found that learning in the harder English→French direction reduced forgetting over a week. Level matters too. Terai, Yamashita and Pasich (2021) found that lower-proficiency learners learned more with L2→L1 practice and higher-proficiency learners with L1→L2. They suggest this explains why earlier studies disagreed, though their study had only 28 learners. Most of these are short lab or classroom studies with paired words.
**Adding the opposite direction (meaning on the front, foreign word on the back):** the research supports it for speaking and writing. Productive practice builds the ability to produce words, which receptive practice alone does much less (Steinel et al., 2007; Webb, 2009). It also brings some receptive knowledge (Webb, 2009; Griffin & Harley, 1996), and its extra difficulty can reduce forgetting (Schneider et al., 2002). For beginners the receptive direction is easier and may work better (Terai et al., 2021), so a sensible design is to keep foreign-word-first cards as the default and add meaning-first cards once a word is known, or for learners who want to speak and write. That ordering is Tamkeen's own reading of these studies from these studies; no study has tested it.

### Memory tricks: a sound-alike word and a picture
**What Tamkeen does:** The Mnemonic maker finds words in your own language that sound like the new word, and links each to the meaning with a picture line (Spanish *pato*, "duck": "Picture a duck balanced on top of a POT"). Keywords that are also close in meaning come first, and your reviews keep testing the word itself.
**What the research says:** The keyword method links a foreign word to a familiar word that sounds like it, then joins that word to the meaning with a vivid image. In the classic study, learners using keywords scored 72% on Russian vocabulary against 46% for a control group (Atkinson & Raugh, 1975). Atkinson (1975) described Russian and Spanish experiments in which keywords beat rote repetition, and Pressley, Levin and Delaney (1982) reviewed the method and its uses. There is an important limit: in several studies keyword learners forgot faster, so the advantage shrank or even reversed after a delay (Wang, Thomas & Ouellette, 1992; Wang & Thomas, 1995). Partly because the benefits seemed limited to short retention periods, one major review rated the method "low utility" (Dunlosky et al., 2013). Pairing keywords with retrieval practice helps. Keyword-plus-retrieval beat keywords alone a week later, and recalling a word through its keyword worked better than recalling it without one (Miyatsu & McDaniel, 2019). With repeated recall, learners gradually stop needing the keyword, and those who switch to recalling the word directly remember it better (Dikmans et al., 2020). This supports combining keyword cards with spaced reviews, as Tamkeen does. On whether keywords related in meaning work better, the evidence is thin. In one study, keywords with a meaningful link were better memory cues than unrelated ones, but experienced language learners in the same study did better with plain repetition than with keywords at all (van Hell & Mahn, 1997). Another study found that keywords that are easy-to-picture nouns help, verb keywords hurt, and for recalling the foreign word the keyword must sound very close to it (Ellis & Beaton, 1993).

### Pictures with words
**What Tamkeen does:** Mnemonic keywords come with a picture, or a picture line to imagine, linking the sound to the meaning, and you still recall the word on the card.
**What the research says:** Dual coding theory holds that the mind stores words and images in two linked systems, so information stored both ways has two routes back into memory (Paivio, 1971, 1986; Clark & Paivio, 1991). In reading studies, words explained with a picture plus text were remembered better than words explained with text alone (Chun & Plass, 1996). A meta-analysis found that text-plus-picture glosses had a moderate effect on vocabulary learning and helped beginners most (Yun, 2011). There is an important limit: in one set of experiments, pictures were no better than translations, because learners became overconfident when they saw a picture. Once that overconfidence was removed, including through retrieval practice, words learned with pictures were remembered better (Carpenter & Olson, 2012). Pictures help most when paired with real recall, as on Tamkeen's cards.

### Every card has an example sentence
**What Tamkeen does:** Cards carry a real sentence using the word, with its translation on the back, so you see how the word is used.
**What the research says:** The research here is more mixed than people expect. An example sentence helps you understand a word in the moment, but studies find it adds little to remembering it. Measuring word knowledge in ten different ways, Webb (2007) found no significant difference between learning words as plain pairs and learning them from a single glossed sentence. Prince (1996) found that learning from translations led to more words remembered than learning from context, and that weaker learners had trouble using the words they had learned in new contexts. Words learned in lists or single sentences were remembered better than words met in a longer text (Laufer & Shmueli, 1997). The strongest result is that recall matters more than context: an informative sentence helped learners understand a word during practice, but practice that made them recall the meaning gave better memory later (van den Broek et al., 2018). Sentences on cards are useful for understanding and for showing real use (a reasonable aim, though not shown to add memory), while the recall step does most of the memory work.

### Sentences you can read
**What Tamkeen does:** When Tamkeen finds an example sentence for a new card, it prefers one where every other word is one you already know, so the new word is the only unknown.
**What the research says:** Krashen's input hypothesis says we acquire language by understanding input that is just a little beyond our current level ("i+1"). It is very influential, but it is a theory that is hard to test directly, and it is still debated. The research on lexical coverage is more concrete: you need to know nearly all the words in a text to understand it without help. In the best-known study, about 98% of the words (roughly one unknown word in 50) had to be known for most learners to understand a story adequately (Hu & Nation, 2000). Later work suggested 98% as the optimal level and 95% as a minimum (Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski, 2010). A study of 661 learners found no sharp threshold: comprehension rose steadily as more words were known (Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe, 2011). A 2023 replication could not fully reproduce the original 98% result (Kremmel et al., 2023), so treat the exact figure with caution. The general principle is solid, though: understanding drops quickly as unknown words pile up. Keeping each new sentence to one unknown word, with its meaning available, fits that principle.

### Learning from real podcasts, videos and books
**What Tamkeen does:** Instead of textbook dialogues, you learn from real podcasts, YouTube, Netflix, reels, audiobooks, books and web pages, with a transcript or captions in the language you're learning.
**What the research says:** Same-language captions help. A meta-analysis of 18 studies found a large effect of captions on both listening comprehension and vocabulary learning (Montero Perez, Van Den Noortgate & Desmet, 2013). Vanderplank's early study of 15 learners found that those above intermediate level got a great deal of usable input from subtitled TV and learned to use the subtitles flexibly, but it was of limited value for low-level learners (Vanderplank, 1988). He later wrote a book reviewing more than 30 years of research on captions (Vanderplank, 2016). Watching ordinary TV teaches new words incidentally, especially words that come up often (Peters & Webb, 2018). However, real TV needs about 3,000 of the most frequent word families, plus names, to understand 95% of the words, and about 7,000 to reach 98% (Webb & Rodgers, 2009). Authentic material therefore works best once learners know the most common words, or with help such as captions and tap-to-translate. Limits: most of the studies are short and measure gains right after viewing.

### Material you choose yourself
**What Tamkeen does:** Nothing is assigned. You learn from the podcasts, channels, shows, books and pages you would pick anyway, about things you already care about, and you can follow a show to keep its new episodes at hand.
**What the research says:** Choice and interest help, though much of the evidence is indirect. A meta-analysis of 41 studies found that being given a choice raised motivation, effort and performance, with the biggest effects for choices that didn't change what was learned (Patall, Cooper & Robinson, 2008); it comes from education in general, not language learning. Among 159 learners of French, those who studied because they valued or enjoyed it felt more competent, were less anxious and more often meant to continue (Noels et al., 2000), though that study measured motivation, not learning. Reading programmes where learners choose their own books work: a meta-analysis of 34 studies found a medium effect over normal classes (Nakanishi, 2015), and one of 49 studies found small-to-medium gains in reading, larger for adults (Jeon & Day, 2016). In the Fiji "book flood", 380 primary pupils given 250 high-interest storybooks per class improved their English reading and listening at twice the normal rate within 8 months (Elley & Mangubhai, 1983). Krashen (2004) reviews many more such studies, as an advocate of free reading rather than a neutral judge. Interest itself helps understanding: 104 advanced readers did better on comprehension questions about texts they found interesting (Brantmeier, 2006), and in a small study of 23 students, interest helped most when the topic was also familiar (Eidswick, 2010), which is what material you choose yourself tends to be. Learners who seek out the language in their free time know more of it: subtitled TV and films (Kuppens, 2010, 374 pupils), TV, the internet and reading (Peters, 2018, where such exposure mattered more than years of lessons), games (Sundqvist & Wikström, 2015) and films above all (Lindgren & Muñoz, 2013). Interest research describes how a passing spark can grow into a lasting interest when it keeps being fed (Hidi & Renninger, 2006). Limits: the free-time studies are correlational (keen learners may simply seek out more), the reading programmes mostly used books simplified for learners, and the choice findings come from education in general.

### Native material, not textbook dialogues
**What Tamkeen does:** The podcasts, videos, books and pages are the real thing, made for native speakers and left unchanged. Tamkeen adds help around them instead: tap a word for its meaning, read along, a translation, new words marked, and courses of the most common words to start with.
**What the research says:** Textbook dialogues are not how people really talk. Compared with similar real conversations, coursebook dialogues differed a lot in turn length, turn-taking, false starts, repetition, pauses, overlapping speech, hesitations and the little "mm-hm"s listeners give (Gilmore, 2004); a later review sets out the case for authentic material and what makes it hard (Gilmore, 2007). In a 10-month study of 62 Japanese university students, the class taught with authentic material beat the textbook class on 5 of 8 tests of communication skills (Gilmore, 2011). In two beginner classes, authentic material kept learners on task and visibly more motivated, even though they rated it less interesting than their usual material (Peacock, 1997). Real TV teaches words: 187 university students who watched ten episodes of one American drama learned new words, and words that came up more often were learned better (Rodgers & Webb, 2020). Staying with one show helps, because a whole season of one programme uses fewer different words than a random mix of programmes and repeats its rarer words far more often (Rodgers & Webb, 2011; an analysis of 288 episodes' scripts, with no learners tested). On the other side, a meta-analysis of 24 studies found that texts written for native speakers taught words more slowly than texts written for learners (an effect of 0.71 against 1.56), and video more slowly than reading or listening (Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa, 2023); real TV also needs a large vocabulary (Webb & Rodgers, 2009, above). Native material works, but it is harder, which is why Tamkeen puts the help around it and starts you on the commonest words. Limits: there is no meta-analysis directly comparing authentic and textbook material, and Gilmore (2011) is one study with whole classes rather than randomly assigned learners.

### Read along while you listen
**What Tamkeen does:** The players show the transcript as the audio plays and light up each word as it is said.
**What the research says:** Reading while listening helps. Over 13 weeks, learners who read graded readers while listening to the audio improved their listening fluency most consistently, compared with reading only or listening only (Chang & Millett, 2014). Audio-assisted reading also raised reading speed and comprehension more than silent reading for beginners (Chang & Millett, 2015). Repeated reading with audio support led to more vocabulary learning than repeated reading without it (Webb & Chang, 2012), and reading and listening to ten graded readers led to sizeable vocabulary gains that were largely kept three months later (Webb & Chang, 2015). Not every study is as positive: in one, learners could recall the meaning of only about 1 in 28 new words met while reading (with or without audio), and none met by listening alone (Brown, Waring & Donkaewbua, 2008). The word-by-word highlighting itself is less proven. In a study of children reading in their own language, highlighting each word as it was spoken changed their eye movements (fewer jumps back) but did not improve learning. The poorest readers liked it most (Gerbier, Bailly & Bosse, 2018). The read-along is well supported; the highlighting is a comfort feature without proven learning gains.

### Tap a word for its meaning
**What Tamkeen does:** Tap any word in a transcript, page, book or caption to see its dictionary form and meaning straight away, and save it as a card.
**What the research says:** Giving readers the meaning of unknown words while they read helps them both understand and learn those words. In a classic study, marginal glosses and dictionaries both helped students learn words that appeared more than once, compared with no help. Dictionary users often skipped the look-up or guessed wrongly, which weakened the benefit (Hulstijn, Hollander & Greidanus, 1996). A one-tap look-up removes most of that effort. A meta-analysis of computer glosses found a medium effect on reading comprehension and a large effect on incidental vocabulary learning (Abraham, 2008). Meanings given in the learner's own language were remembered better than meanings given in English (Laufer & Shmueli, 1997). Glosses with both text and a picture worked best for comprehension, and every type of gloss helped learners notice and recognise more target words than no gloss (Yanguas, 2009).

### The most common words first
**What Tamkeen does:** Each language's Basics course is its 1,000 most common words, with sentences and natural audio, and those words are the first to become cards.
**What the research says:** Learning words in order of frequency pays off because the commonest words come up again and again in everything you read and hear. Using frequency lists from a large corpus, Nation (2006) estimated that understanding written English without help (98% of words known) needs about 8,000–9,000 word families, and spoken English about 6,000–7,000. Schmitt and Schmitt (2014) argue that the top 3,000 word families should count as "high-frequency" vocabulary and be taught first. The first 1,000 words are the natural starting point on that path. Even TV needs about the top 3,000 families for 95% coverage (Webb & Rodgers, 2009). Limits: these figures come from English corpora. The principle carries over to other languages, but the exact numbers will differ.
**Studies:**

### Grammar explained clearly
**What Tamkeen does:** The Grammar courses explain each point step by step, in the language you speak, with examples you can hear.
**What the research says:** Teaching grammar explicitly works. A meta-analysis of 49 studies found that focused instruction produced large gains that lasted, and that explicit instruction (explaining the rule) worked better than implicit instruction (Norris & Ortega, 2000). A later meta-analysis of 41 studies found explicit instruction beat implicit instruction for both simple and complex grammar points, and helped learners use the forms in spontaneous speech and writing as well as in controlled exercises (Spada & Tomita, 2010). Limits: Norris and Ortega warn that the kind of test used affects how large the effects look, and the studies define "explicit" in different ways. There is no solid research on whether grammar explanations work better in the learner's own language than in the target language, so Tamkeen makes no claim about the language of the explanations.
**Studies:**
- Norris, J. M., & Ortega, L. (2000). Effectiveness of L2 instruction: A research synthesis and quantitative meta-analysis. *Language Learning, 50*(3), 417–528. [https://doi.org/10.1111/0023-8333.00136](https://doi.org/10.1111/0023-8333.00136)

### A game around the learning
**What Tamkeen does:** Studying earns XP and coins for your creatures, which hatch from eggs and fight in live battles and gym battles where each move is a word question.
**What the research says:** Gamification has small but real benefits. A meta-analysis found significant effects on learning (g = 0.49), motivation (g = 0.36) and behaviour (g = 0.25). The learning effect held up in the best-designed studies; the motivation and behaviour effects were less stable (Sailer & Homner, 2020). A meta-analysis of serious games found they taught more than ordinary instruction and helped retention, but were not more motivating (Wouters et al., 2013). A review of gamified English-learning studies found positive experiences and outcomes, but no study could say which game elements caused the learning (Dehghanzadeh et al., 2021). Limits: results vary a lot between studies. Game features help most as a wrapper around proven methods such as recall and spacing, not as a replacement for them.
**Studies:**

### Daily streaks
**What Tamkeen does:** Home shows your daily flashcard streak, and the game's goal streak can be protected with a freeze, so one missed day doesn't wipe out your progress.
**What the research says:** Doing something daily in the same situation gradually makes it automatic. In a 12-week study, 96 volunteers repeated a daily behaviour at the same point in their day. How automatic it felt rose along a curve that levelled off after anywhere from 18 to 254 days, depending on the person. Missing a single day did not materially affect the process (Lally et al., 2010). On streaks specifically, seven studies found that showing people an unbroken streak made them more likely to keep going than showing them a broken one. Losing a streak hurt more when people blamed themselves, and less when they could "repair" it (Silverman & Barasch, 2023). Streaks motivate, but a broken streak can make people quit, so a forgiving design (a missed day isn't fatal, or a broken streak can be repaired) fits the research. That design point is Tamkeen's reading from these two studies.
**Studies:**
- Lally, P., van Jaarsveld, C. H. M., Potts, H. W. W., & Wardle, J. (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. *European Journal of Social Psychology, 40*(6), 998–1009. [https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)

### Marking words you know
**What Tamkeen does:** Words you've met are coloured as you read: amber means not known yet. Hold a word to mark it known, so you notice what you still need to learn.
**What the research says:** Schmidt's "noticing hypothesis" argues that language input only becomes learning when you consciously notice it, and that paying attention to what is to be learned helps (Schmidt, 1990). Marking a word as known or not yet known makes you stop and notice it. This is a theoretical argument, and it is debated. Ticking the words you know is also a recognised way to measure vocabulary: the yes/no test format (Meara & Buxton, 1987) does exactly this, and adds made-up words to catch people who overclaim. Limits: there is no study testing whether marking words while reading improves learning, so this feature rests on theory rather than direct evidence. Self-marking can also be over-generous (see the section on two answer buttons).
**Studies:**
- Schmidt, R. W. (1990). The role of consciousness in second language learning. *Applied Linguistics, 11*(2), 129–158. [https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/11.2.129](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/11.2.129)

### Studying with friends
**What Tamkeen does:** Friends can see each other's streaks and what they are studying, chat, join guilds and challenge each other's creatures.
**What the research says:** Tracking your progress helps you reach goals, and it helps more when others can see it. A meta-analysis of 138 experiments (nearly 20,000 people) found that prompting people to monitor their progress improved goal attainment (d = 0.40). The effect was larger when progress was reported to others or made public, and when it was physically recorded (Harkin et al., 2016). In gamified learning, combining competition with collaboration was especially effective for changing behaviour (Sailer & Homner, 2020), and educational games produced larger learning gains when people played in groups (Wouters et al., 2013). Limits: none of this research tests friends in a language app specifically. The evidence is about goal tracking and group play in general.

### Hearing every word
**What Tamkeen does:** Cards play the audio they were saved from when it can be kept, and otherwise a natural voice reads the word and the sentence aloud.
**What the research says:** Hearing words helps you learn their sound, and hearing them in several voices helps more. When English speakers learned Spanish words, hearing each word from several speakers led to better learning than hearing it from one (Barcroft & Sommers, 2005). Seeing the written word while hearing it improved later recognition of the words compared with sound or text alone (Bird & Williams, 2002). You learn the form you are exposed to: learners who saw captions got better at recognising words in writing, while those who heard the audio got better at recognising them by ear (Sydorenko, 2010). Memory for speech sounds is thought to be a key tool for learning new words (Baddeley, Gathercole & Papagno, 1998). Limits: these are mostly short lab studies. There is no study comparing recorded native speakers with synthetic voices.
**Studies:**

### Quick questions in the battle game
**What Tamkeen does:** In live battles you answer a word question within a few seconds to land a move. The review cards themselves are never timed out: their time bar is only a guide.
**What the research says:** There is no study testing a short answer timer like this one. Bjork's "desirable difficulties" idea says some things that make practice feel harder, such as spacing and testing yourself, improve long-term memory (Bjork, 1994; Bjork & Bjork, 2011). Effortful recall that still succeeds is better for memory than easy recall (Pyc & Rawson, 2009). But the difficulty has to be one the learner can overcome; otherwise it is simply undesirable (Bjork & Bjork, 2011). Pressure can backfire: when quizzes were framed as high-stakes, they lost their advantage over rereading, while low-stakes quizzes kept it (Hinze & Rapp, 2014). Taken together, the timer is best treated as a fun game mechanic, not a learning booster, and the main reviews should stay untimed. That recommendation is Tamkeen's own reading of these studies.

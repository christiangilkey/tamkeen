# What is Tamkeen

Tamkeen (تمكين, "empowerment" in Arabic) is an app for learning a language from real material: podcasts, videos, shows, reels, books and web pages, in the language you're learning. Every word in them can be tapped, saved as a flashcard and reviewed until you know it, and a creature game turns the studying into play. It runs on your computer, Android, iPhone and the web, and keeps them all in step through your own Google Drive.

This page has four parts: what Tamkeen can do, the inventive ways it does it, why it is designed the way it is (with the research behind each choice, including where the evidence is weak), and every study it cites.

---

## Part 1 · What Tamkeen can do

### Languages
- **Languages you can learn**: Arabic, Spanish, French, Japanese, Chinese, Russian, English, Italian, Tagalog, Brazilian Portuguese, Catalan, Bisaya (Cebuano), Thai, German, Persian (Farsi), Hindi, Korean and Latin, 18 in all.
- **Languages the app can be shown in**: English, Spanish, Arabic, French, Japanese, Chinese, Russian, Italian, Tagalog, Brazilian Portuguese, Catalan, Bisaya (Cebuano), Thai, German, Persian (Farsi), Hindi and Korean, 17 in all (you can learn Latin, but the app isn't shown in it).
- **Arabic dialects**: when you learn Arabic, the courses teach Standard Arabic (MSA) or the Levantine, Egyptian, Gulf, Moroccan or Iraqi dialect, whichever you pick.
- **Learn several languages**: tick the languages you're learning in Settings, and the switch in the top bar moves between them, each showing only its own content and words.
- **Everything follows your language**: the language you pick decides what the speech recognition listens for, what the AI explains, which way the text runs (right to left for Arabic and Persian), its font, and what podcasts, YouTube, news, books and Netflix offer you.
- **Content ready for each language**: every language comes with podcasts, YouTube topics, news sites, free books, free audiobooks (none for Thai yet), a list of its 1,000 most common words and a built-in dictionary.
- **Every form counts as one word**: in Arabic, Spanish, French, Italian, Russian, English, Tagalog and Latin, all forms of a word count as the same word, so "ran" and "running" both count as "run" (Spanish commands with pronouns attached and "vos" forms included).
- **Real words in Japanese, Chinese and Thai**: text written without spaces is split into real dictionary words, so you tap whole words rather than single characters.
- **Arabic vowel marks**: a Show vowels switch puts the vowel marks on transcripts, web pages, reels and review cards, added by your computer and shown on your phone once it syncs.
- **Japanese and Chinese readings**: furigana show above Japanese words and pinyin above Chinese ones, and you choose simplified or traditional Chinese characters.
- **Thai pronunciation guide**: a guide to how each Thai word is said sits above it, and you can turn it off.
- **Russian stress marks**: a switch shows which syllable is stressed, and words written with е or ё are treated as the same.
- **English pronunciation**: the word popup shows the American pronunciation of English words.
- **Latin with or without long marks**: Latin words are recognised whether or not they are written with macrons, and search ignores them.

### Getting around
- **Home**: Home greets you with your daily streak, one big Continue button that picks up where you left off, and your creature's habitat.
- **Five main tabs**: Home, Library, Vocab, Friends and Settings hold everything, with a badge on Friends for new requests.
- **Top bar**: the top bar of every screen has the language switch, a refresh button and a round ? button.
- **Help on every screen**: the ? button outlines each button on the screen in turn with a line on what it does (the ? key does the same on a computer), and in the players an animated fingertip shows how to tap a word or slide across several.
- **First-time guides**: a short guide shows the first time you open Home, the Library, Vocab or Friends, and Settings can show them again.
- **First-run questions**: when you first open Tamkeen it asks the language you speak, the one you're learning, your level and (if you want one) an AI key with step-by-step help, and if you've already started learning, the 1,000 most common words count as already seen.
- **Search**: one search box looks through all your transcripts and pages, ignoring Arabic vowel marks and Latin long marks, and a result opens at its paragraph.
- **Swipe between tabs**: on the phones and in the web app, swipe left or right to move between sections.
- **Fix-it popups**: a problem you can put right yourself (the microphone not allowed, a missing AI key, a sign-in) shows one popup with a button that takes you straight to the fix.
- **Troubleshoot**: when an AI or transcription key fails, a Troubleshoot button lists the likely causes and what to do about each.
- **The same look everywhere**: the app's fonts, menus and popups are its own, so every computer and phone shows the same thing, also offline.

### Learn from real material

**The Library**
- **One place for everything**: the Library holds Courses, Podcasts, YouTube, Netflix, Reels, Audiobooks, Books, My webpages, My videos and My audio, and opens on the kind you looked at last.
- **One + button**: the + button opens straight on the way to add the kind you're looking at, with "All the ways to add" for the full list, and an empty section points you to it.
- **Continue where you left off**: each kind remembers what you were watching, reading or hearing, so you can carry on.
- **Delete selected**: tick items and delete them, whole episodes included, from all your devices at once.
- **Send to a friend**: anything in your Library (podcasts, videos, pages, books, reels) can be sent to a friend, and its transcript goes with it.

**Podcasts**
- **Follow podcasts**: follow any podcast and its new episodes arrive in your Library, on every device (29 Latin podcasts among them).
- **Trending podcasts**: the Discover page lists popular podcasts in the language you're learning, from Apple's charts, with hand-picked shows for languages the charts miss, such as Bisaya.

**YouTube**
- **Open YouTube**: a menu of topics (news, music, comedy, cooking, travel and more) searches YouTube in the language you're learning, and starts on what's popular in a country you choose.
- **Tap a video to study it**: a video you pick in YouTube plays in Tamkeen's own player, with its captions as the transcript and every word tappable.
- **Choose your captions**: show only videos whose subtitles were written by a person, or let a video without them use YouTube's automatic captions or its translation.
- **Captions written by Gemini**: for a public video of up to an hour with no captions at all, your own Gemini key can watch it and write the captions, for a few US cents.
- **Other soundtracks**: the player tells you when a video can also be heard in the language you're learning, and where to choose that soundtrack.
- **Follow channels**: follow a YouTube channel from the player or its page, and the channels you follow are listed under YouTube on all your devices.

**Netflix**
- **Netflix with tappable subtitles**: Open Netflix browses titles in the language you're learning, and a title you pick plays with its subtitles as tappable words and a transcript *(desktop app on Windows, and the web app in Chrome or Edge with the "Tamkeen for Netflix" extension)*.
- **Choose soundtrack and subtitles**: Audio and Subtitles buttons switch Netflix's soundtrack and subtitles (for example Arabic subtitles in Standard Arabic or a dialect), the transcript and look-ups follow your choice, and a change of the language you're learning carries over to Netflix.
- **Netflix titles on your phone**: titles watched on the computer arrive in your phone's Library with their subtitles to read.

**Instagram reels**
- **Reels in your language**: the computer finds popular reels in the language you're learning and turns their speech into words, and they play one after another with the words underneath.
- **Your own Instagram saves**: get reels from your saved Instagram posts, all of them or one collection, and tick the ones you want *(desktop app and phones)*.
- **Random reels**: get a chosen number of random reels from all of Instagram in the language you're learning *(desktop app and phones)*.
- **Add a reel by its link**: in the web app, a public reel can be added from its link *(web app)*.
- **Reel player**: reels show big captions, new words in purple, a pause-or-keep-playing switch while you look words up, and the next or previous reel.

**Audiobooks**
- **Free audiobooks**: LibriVox's free public-domain audiobooks in your language are listed with cover, author, length and chapters, most popular first, with a search.
- **Chapter by chapter**: an audiobook's first chapter is transcribed straight away, and "Next chapter" goes on to the next, which is transcribed while you listen.
- **Your own audiobook files**: add one audiobook file or several (one per chapter becomes one book), including DRM-free M4B files from shops such as Libro.fm.

**Books**
- **Free bookshelf**: about 90 famous public-domain books in every language (16 in Latin), read one full page at a time with every word tappable and their bold, italics and headings kept.
- **Your own books**: upload EPUB, PDF, Kindle, Word or plain-text files and read them the same way.

**Web pages**
- **Import a web page**: paste an address, pick one of your saved sites or choose a news site in your language, and read the page with every word tappable, title included, at its own text size.
- **Built-in web browser**: browse inside the app with a start page, starred sites kept on all your devices, and a round "Import to Tamkeen" button *(desktop app and phones; the web app has a page of popular sites instead)*.
- **Share a page to Tamkeen**: on Android, share a page from another app straight to Tamkeen's web app.

**Your own files**
- **My videos**: add your own video files, which are kept in your Google Drive, transcribed and played with tappable captions over the video.
- **My audio**: add your own audio files (MP3, M4A, M4B, AAC, OGG, Opus, FLAC, WAV, WMA, AMR, WebM, MKA or AIFF), transcribed and played like a podcast.

### Transcription and read-along
- **Speech turned into text**: podcasts, videos, reels and audiobooks are written down with every word timed to the sound.
- **On your own computer**: the desktop app writes transcripts itself, and Settings helps you pick a speech model that suits your computer *(desktop app)*.
- **In the cloud**: with your own Groq or OpenAI key, transcripts are written online, so a computer that can't run a big model, or a phone on its own, can transcribe.
- **Ask your computer from your phone**: your phone or the web app can ask for an episode to be transcribed, and your computer picks the request up.
- **Thai speech model**: Thai is transcribed on the computer by Pathumma, a model trained on Thai by Thailand's national electronics centre, with about a quarter fewer mistakes *(desktop app; the Mac uses the standard model)*.
- **Bisaya speech model**: a speech model made for Bisaya writes much better Bisaya transcripts, on the computer only, since cloud services can't do Bisaya *(desktop app)*.
- **How accurate each model is**: for Thai and Bisaya, Settings shows how the language's own model compares with the cloud model.
- **Progress bars**: while your computer transcribes, two bars show it finding the speech and then writing it down, each with its percentage.
- **Misheard words put right**: when a word lookup spots a word the transcript got wrong, it shows the right word and corrects the transcript, with Undo.
- **Read along**: the player lights up each word as it is said and scrolls the text along with it, and pressing play brings you back to the word being said.
- **Slow down and loop**: play slower, loop a sentence, and hear a line again.
- **Skip back and forward**: jump 5 seconds back or forward, to the previous or next line, or back to the start of the sentence (press again to go one sentence further back).
- **Captions and transcript**: videos show tappable captions over the picture, and a transcript panel slides in beside them or folds away with a handle.

### Word lookup and dictionaries
- **Tap any word**: tap a word in a transcript, caption, page, book or reel to hear just that word, pause the audio, and see what it means in this sentence.
- **Look up a phrase**: press on a word and drag (or hold and slide your finger) across up to 30 words, and the popup explains them as one phrase in its sentences.
- **New words in purple**: words you've never looked at show in purple, and looking at or saving one form of a word marks all its forms as seen.
- **Hold to mark known**: press and hold a purple word and let go to mark it known without opening the popup, with Undo; hold a known word to make it new again.
- **"Not known yet"**: the ? button in the word popup marks a word amber everywhere, in all its forms, without saving it or counting it as known.
- **Word menu**: keep holding a word (or right-click it) for Save, Copy, Play from here, and Mark as seen or new.
- **Explanations by AI**: with your own Claude, Gemini, OpenAI or Grok key, an AI explains the word using the sentences around it, says how much it used, and keeps the answer so the same word isn't asked about twice.
- **Built-in dictionary**: with no AI at all, meanings come from a built-in dictionary (Wiktionary) for every language, with a second Arabic dictionary of about 39,000 Standard and Egyptian words and fuller Bisaya dictionaries.
- **Dictionary first**: by default the dictionary answers first and the AI only when the dictionary has nothing, and the dictionary downloads by itself when it's missing.
- **Where each meaning came from**: a tag at the top right of the popup says where the meaning came from, tapping it changes the source, and if the AI fails the popup offers "Use the dictionary instead".
- **Quick or detailed**: a switch in every player shows either just the meaning or the full explanation.
- **Popup buttons**: the popup plays the word or its whole sentence, carries on playing with Continue, and saves the word as a flashcard with its sentence and sound in one tap.
- **Add audio**: when a saved word wouldn't get a voice by itself, an Add audio button takes you to the voice settings.
- **Vibration**: a light vibration when you tap or save a word, and a small celebration on saving *(the phones; can be turned off)*.

### Vocabulary and flashcard review

**Your words**
- **Vocab page**: all the words you've saved, each showing where it came from, opening as a card with a tap.
- **Folders**: put words in folders (one word can be in several), and add or remove many words at once.
- **Sort, filter and export**: sort your words, filter them by date, Select all, and export exactly the words on screen as a spreadsheet file.

**Flashcards**
- **Review on a schedule**: saved words come back as flashcards on a schedule timed to your memory, answered Remembered or Not remembered by a tap, a swipe right or left, a key or a controller.
- **The front never gives it away**: the front of a card never shows the word being asked, even if the meaning mentions it.
- **Meaning and translation**: the back shows the word with its meaning under it, and the sentence with its translation under it, in your own language.
- **Fix a card as you go**: tap the word, meaning, sentence or translation on the back to change it right there (or Copy it), and the change reaches your other devices.
- **Highlight the right words**: hold a word in the card's sentence to mark it as part of what you're learning (a two-part verb, a whole phrase), or take a wrong highlight away.
- **Notes**: each card has Grammar notes and Memorization notes, with a Paste button.
- **Say it**: record yourself saying the word, and hear the real sentence, a short pause, then your recording to compare.
- **Undo and delete**: undo an answer, or delete a word from its card.
- **Review timers**: a time bar on each card, a clock for the whole session and an estimate of the time left help you keep going.
- **Daily goal bar**: a bar under the Review box shows today's new words and reviews done and about how long the rest will take, and tapping it sets how many new words you want a day.
- **Flashcard streak**: a gold flame shows how many days in a row you met your flashcard goal, on Home and beside your name for friends.
- **Cards left on the app icon**: the number of cards left today shows on the app's icon (the taskbar or Dock on a computer, a quiet notification and a home-screen widget on Android, the icon on iPhone, and the web app once added to the home screen).

**Anki and planning**
- **Export for Anki**: make a ready-to-import Anki deck with each word's audio and its review progress, a deck for each language, with card fronts that never give the word away.
- **Two-way Anki sync**: keep your words and reviews in step with Anki on the computer or AnkiDroid on Android *(desktop app and Android; off until you turn it on)*.
- **Anki reviews count in the game**: an Anki add-on lets reviews done in Anki on the same computer count in the creature game *(desktop app)*.
- **Plan today's review**: put today's review in Google Calendar at a time you choose, or send it as a to-do to Google Tasks or Onyx Calendar, with how long it should take.

### Making cards

**Add words**
- **Add words**: type new words one by one, or paste a list of up to 100 (straight from a spreadsheet too), and Tamkeen makes cards of them.
- **Filled in for you**: Tamkeen can add each word's meaning (from the dictionary or your AI), a short example sentence with its translation, a voice and a memory trick, never replaces what you typed, points out words you already have, and lets you look it all over before adding.
- **Your own audio**: record or choose your own audio for any card instead.
- **Scan words from a photo**: photograph a word list, a page of a book or a single word (or choose or paste a picture), and Tamkeen reads the words and their meanings into new cards.
- **Scanning with or without AI**: your AI reads print and handwriting, and without AI or offline the photo is read on the device itself, best with printed text, and photos are never kept.

**Mnemonics**
- **Mnemonic maker**: on the back of a card, the Mnemonic maker suggests words that sound like the word you're learning, each with a picture line that ties it to the meaning.
- **How the word is really said**: with an AI key, it first shows how the word is said, in syllables with the stressed one in capitals, then checks each suggestion really sounds like it.
- **Close or partial, and fits the meaning**: each suggestion says whether it's a close or partial sound match, ones that also point to the meaning come first, and long words get keywords for each part or of two or three words.
- **Use or thumbs down**: Use adds a keyword and its picture to Memorization notes, and thumbs down drops it so it's never suggested for that word again.
- **Mnemonics without AI**: with no AI key, the Mnemonic maker finds sound-alike words from a pronunciation dictionary, in English or in Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Russian or Catalan when the app is shown in one of those.
- **It learns your taste**: the free Mnemonic maker learns what kinds of keywords you like from Use and thumbs down, on all your devices, and Settings can make it forget.
- **Mnemonic settings**: choose the Mnemonic maker's AI model and how hard it thinks, with its cost shown (about 2 US cents a press at most).

**Example sentences and card voices**
- **Natural example sentences**: with an AI key, example sentences are written the way a native speaker really talks, in the word's own register (slang stays slang).
- **Real sentences without AI**: with no AI key, Tamkeen finds a real sentence that uses the word, with its translation, preferring one whose other words you already know.
- **Dialect sentences**: when you study an Arabic dialect, example sentences and mnemonic sounds come from that dialect where there are any, and Bisaya has about 20,000 extra sentences from a Cebuano dictionary.
- **Card voices**: cards without their own recording are read out by your device's own voice (free), natural Azure voices with your own key (Arabic by country), or voices made on your computer that reach your phone when it syncs.
- **A voice the moment you save**: with Azure, a word saved from YouTube, Netflix, a reel, a page or a book gets its voice straight away, saying the word and its whole sentence.
- **Latin card voice**: Latin cards are read by an Italian voice using the Church pronunciation, which you can turn off.

### Courses
- **Courses in the Library**: Library → Courses holds a Grammar course and a Basics course for the language you're learning (every language but Latin).
- **Basics**: the 1,000 most common words of the language, 50 at a time, each with an everyday sentence, its translation and audio of both.
- **Add to your cards**: add one basics word, or a whole unit, to your cards in one tap, with its audio, and it goes into your reviews.
- **Grammar from zero**: 45 to 54 lessons for people who know no grammar, each named after something you'll be able to do ("Say what you want", "Tell a story"), from the very beginning to everyday conversation.
- **How a lesson works**: each lesson opens with the sentence you'll be able to say and a short conversation, explains the grammar in plain words with examples and common mistakes, and ends with practice.
- **Many kinds of practice**: multiple choice, fill the gap, put the words in order, choose your replies in a conversation, say it aloud, and "your turn", which your AI can check.
- **Mixed review**: from the fourth lesson some questions come back from earlier lessons, and Mixed review asks questions from all the lessons you've done.
- **Hear every example**: tap any example in a lesson to hear it in your card voice.
- **Readings in Latin letters**: in Japanese, Chinese, Korean, Thai, Hindi, Persian, Russian and Arabic, every example shows how it is said in Latin letters, with "Hide readings" to switch it off.
- **Grammar for learning English**: English grammar courses explained in your own language, for speakers of each of the 16 other languages Tamkeen is shown in, in American English.
- **Explained in your language**: courses are explained in English or the language you're learning, and for any other pair your AI can translate a lesson's explanations into your language.
- **Arabic dialect courses**: a Dialect menu offers Standard Arabic, Levantine, Egyptian, Gulf, Moroccan and Iraqi, each with grammar lessons on what it does differently and a Basics list of its own common words, with audio by a voice of that dialect (none yet for Moroccan).
- **Progress and XP**: finished lessons show their score, earn XP for your creature, and sync between your devices.

### The creature game

**Your creatures**
- **An egg that grows as you study**: an egg on Home hatches and grows as you study, from egg to hatchling to juvenile, until it is fully grown.
- **18 creatures**: six elements (Ember, Tide, Grove, Spark, Stone and Dusk), each with three hand-drawn pixel-art creatures (an original, a cyberpunk and a fantasy one) that walk, blink, dip their heads and sleep.
- **Baby looks**: hatchlings have a baby look of their own, smaller with a bigger head, until they grow into juveniles.
- **Fully grown**: a fully grown creature leaves you a new egg and 25 coins, and can be released in the garden.
- **Special colourings**: Twin, Nocturne and Champion colourings, earned by how you study and battle, change how a creature looks.
- **Creatures page**: choose each creature's four battle moves and colouring, and which one friends see.
- **Pet your creature**: petting your creature shows one of your saved words and its meaning in a bubble.
- **Badges**: permanent badges record what you've done, such as streaks, saved words, filled study habits and a fully grown creature.

**Earning**
- **XP for studying**: looking up words (with surprise bonuses and combos), saving words, study time, flashcards and reaching your daily goal all earn XP.
- **XP from courses**: finished grammar lessons, examples you tap to hear and basics words you add to your cards earn XP too.
- **Daily goal and goal streak**: reaching your daily study goal (minutes of listening or reading) pays the most and builds a goal streak, which freezes can protect.
- **Levels, coins and more**: you earn levels, coins, gems, glow and hearts, and tapping any of them in the habitat's bar explains what it's for.
- **Flashcards and battles together**: flashcards and battles pay their own rewards, with a bonus for doing both in a day.

**Shop and garden**
- **Shop**: a daily selection of wearables (113 in all: hats, glasses, scarves, necklaces, things to hold, capes, packs and shirts) that fit each creature's shape, in smaller versions for hatchlings, and decorations for the habitat.
- **Battle moves in the shop**: four battle moves are on offer each day for 40 to 130 coins, bought once and usable by any of your creatures.
- **The garden**: build buildings that produce while you're away, feed your creatures, and buy supplies such as potions, XP boosts, battle tickets, an incubator for a second egg and mystery eggs.

**Battles**
- **Battles from your own words**: once you've saved 100 words, battles quiz you on your own words.
- **Live arena battles**: your creature runs around a big arena made fresh for each battle (meadows, forests, shores, deserts, volcanoes, caves, ruins, night fields or snow), with a small map and an arrow pointing to your rival.
- **Move how you like**: move with a controller's stick or D-pad, W A S D or the arrow keys, by clicking or holding the mouse, or by dragging a finger.
- **Every move asks a word**: each of your four moves (shown as banners placed like a controller's X Y A B buttons) pauses the battle for a word question with a 5-second bar, where a right answer lands the move, quick ones build a combo, and a wrong or slow one fails.
- **The ground matters**: some ground slows you, is slippery, burns, heals or helps one element, and some moves change the ground.
- **Elements**: each element is strong against two others, so picking the right creature matters.
- **Which words**: "Last 100 words" asks your 100 newest words with every creature at the same level, and "All-time" asks any saved word, with creatures growing stronger the more words you know.
- **Kinds of battle**: wild encounters (with potions), rental duels with this week's shared creatures that count for your skill rating, gyms, and challenges against friends' teams.
- **Gyms**: beat ten gym leaders in order, from Pip to Zenith the champion, each harder than the last with less time for each question, opening the next and paying coins and XP the first time, with rematches any time.
- **Weekly rules**: friend battles have a rule each week, such as "Basic moves only", "No healing" or "One on one".
- **Sound effects switch**: Settings can turn off the game's chimes and jingles while keeping the game.
- **Game off switch**: a Game features switch in Settings turns the whole creature game off or on.

**Playing with friends**
- **Challenge friends**: challenge a friend's team to a battle.
- **Leagues**: climb from Bronze to Silver, Gold and Diamond.
- **Guild raids**: wear down a weekly boss together with your guild.
- **Gifts**: send gifts to friends.
- **Friends see your game**: beside each friend's name are their language, league, today's XP, flashcard streak and favourite creature, and tapping any part explains it.

### Friends, chat and guilds
- **An optional account**: connect with Google and pick a username, with a "Sign in with Google" button on the Friends tab.
- **Friends**: add friends by username or with Find a friend, accept or decline requests, and see changes live.
- **Profiles**: profile photos, a profile card that folds away, and an online dot only friends can see.
- **Chat**: text messages and voice notes of up to 2 minutes, with unread counts and notifications on every version.
- **Disappearing messages**: messages disappear once they're seen, played or added, or after 30 days unopened.
- **Share words**: send saved words or a whole folder to a friend, with a short audio clip of each sentence, and they add them in one tap.
- **Share what you're watching**: send anything from your Library to a friend, and it arrives with its words ready.
- **What I'm studying**: a card on your profile shows up to three things you're studying (a podcast episode, a YouTube video, a reel, a web page, or a Spotify, Apple Music or IMDb link), which friends open in Tamkeen's players.
- **Feed, Friends and Guilds**: the Friends tab has a Feed of friends' study cards, your friends and requests, and your guilds.
- **Guilds**: create a guild with a name and tag, invite friends, and join as many as you like.

### Sync across devices and Google Drive
- **Your library in your own Drive**: podcasts, transcripts, saved words, explanations, review progress and game progress are kept in a private folder in your own Google Drive, so all your devices share one library.
- **Within seconds**: changes reach your other devices within seconds, and every version has a refresh button.
- **Settings travel too**: your AI keys, controller buttons, Instagram collections, followed channels and starred sites reach your other devices.
- **The same audio everywhere**: each transcribed episode keeps a small copy of its audio, so other devices play exactly what was transcribed.
- **Delete everywhere at once**: deleting an episode, video or file removes it from every device.
- **One Google sign-in**: in the web app, one Google sign-in covers both Drive sync and Friends, and renews itself.
- **Backups**: export your library to a backup file, import one, or open the data folder *(desktop app)*.
- **Reset Tamkeen**: start the app again as if it were new, without deleting anything, and everything comes back when you sign in again.
- **Your keys stay yours**: your AI keys are kept on your own devices and your own Drive, left out of backups, and photos you scan go only to your own AI provider.

### Settings and personal choices
- **Settings in sections**: Settings has nine sections (Languages, Word definitions, Listening & transcripts, Card voices, Look & feel, Controller, Account & sync, Apps & connections, Data & updates), each showing its current setting.
- **Search Settings**: a search box finds any setting and opens it, and "More options" holds the expert settings.
- **Theme and text size**: choose a light, dark or system theme and the size of the app's text *(the web app is always dark)*.
- **Changelog and About**: Settings shows the changelog of every version, an About page and the open-source licences.

### Controller and accessibility
- **Game controller everywhere**: a USB or Bluetooth controller (Xbox, PlayStation, Nintendo Switch Pro and most others) works on every screen except the built-in web browser, and does nothing while you type in a text box.
- **Like the Xbox dashboard**: the stick or D-pad moves a highlight, A selects, B goes back, X and Y do the screen's extra things, the bumpers switch tabs, the triggers jump a page, and Menu opens more options.
- **Controller in the players**: play and pause, skip, change line, hear a line again, loop, slow down, and move a highlight word by word to open, play and save words.
- **Controller on review cards**: turn the card, answer with the bumpers, play the word or sentence, Say it, walk through the card's boxes, and press the left stick for the Mnemonic maker.
- **Controller on Netflix's pages**: move between Netflix's titles and buttons, pick, go back and scroll, with a small guide in the corner.
- **Button guide**: a strip at the foot of the screen shows what each button does there, and a small controller button in the corner shows or hides it.
- **Choose your own buttons**: Settings → Controller shows a drawing of your controller with a line from each button to its job on the screen you pick, names buttons as printed on your controller, and lets any button be given another job.
- **Keyboard shortcuts**: on a computer, keys work the players and the battles, such as R for back to the start of the sentence and ? for help.
- **Calm options**: sound effects, vibration and the whole game can each be switched off.

### Where Tamkeen runs
- **Desktop app**: for Windows, Mac and Linux, and the only version that transcribes on the device itself.
- **Android app**: installed from a file attached to each release.
- **iPhone app**: installed with AltStore rather than the App Store, and updated with every release.
- **Web app**: Tamkeen's phone screens as a website in any browser, which can be added to your home screen.
- **Every feature in every version**: all four versions do the same things, apart from what a device can't do.
- **Updates**: "Update now" downloads and installs a new version by itself *(desktop app and Android)*, the iPhone explains how to update with AltStore, and the web app shows what's new the first time a new version opens.
- **Inside other apps**: other apps such as Onyx Calendar can open a YouTube video straight in Tamkeen's player, and on the computer Tamkeen's player can appear inside them *(not in the web app)*.

## Part 2 · How it does it: inventive solutions

These are some of the hard problems solved while building Tamkeen, and how they were solved.

### Free and private by design
- **Speech recognition on your own computer.** *Paid transcription services charge by the hour and send your audio away.* Tamkeen runs Whisper, a free speech-recognition model, on your own computer, and it writes down every word with the exact moment it is said, so the player can light up each word as you hear it. If your computer is too slow, you can use a cloud service with your own key instead: Tamkeen first finds the speech, cuts it at pauses and sends only that, so you never pay for silence or music (about nine hours of audio for a dollar).
- **A dictionary first, AI only when it's needed.** *Explaining words with AI needs a paid key that many learners don't have.* Every language has a large built-in dictionary, made from Wiktionary plus scholarly Arabic and Bisaya dictionaries, that downloads once and then works offline. By default the dictionary answers first and the AI is asked only about words it lacks; a small tag on every word popup says where the meaning came from, and a tap on it switches the source.
- **Memory tricks found by sound, with no AI.** *The "keyword method" needs a word in your own language that sounds like the foreign one, and AI often suggests words that don't sound alike at all.* Tamkeen breaks both words into speech sounds using a pronunciation dictionary and measures how close they really are, giving extra weight to the stressed syllable, so only true sound-alikes get through (Spanish *pato*, "duck", gives "pot"). On 38 words with published keyword examples, all 39 good keywords passed and all 42 bad ones failed; a map of related meanings then puts keywords that also hint at the meaning first (German *Hund* gives "hound").
- **Example sentences you can actually read.** *A sentence full of unknown words teaches very little.* For each card, Tamkeen searches a downloaded collection of real sentences with translations and prefers one where every other word is one you have already met, counting every form of a word as the same word. For a learner who knows 1,500 common words, the share of readable sentences rose from about a third to four in five.
- **A voice for every card, three ways.** *Cards saved from YouTube, Netflix, web pages or books have no sound you are allowed to keep.* Each card can be read by your device's own free voice, by Microsoft's natural-sounding Azure voices on your own free account (called straight from your device, with nothing in between), or by a free voice program on your computer whose recordings sync to your phone. Voices with non-commercial licences were left out, and the word and its sentence are joined into one small sound file without being re-encoded.
- **A word list from a photo.** *Typing up a vocabulary list from a textbook is slow.* Photograph the page and Tamkeen reads it, either with your AI or entirely on the device with an open-source text reader that downloads once (about 6 MB for Spanish and English). It rebuilds the page's columns into "word – meaning" pairs, drops headings and page numbers, flags shaky readings for you to check, and never keeps the photo (on Android it even deletes the copy the camera app leaves behind).
- **Vowel marks for Arabic.** *Arabic is usually written without its short vowels, which makes reading aloud hard for learners.* A small open model on your computer adds them to whole transcripts in about 3 seconds per hour of audio, and they sync to your phone; only what is drawn on screen changes, so tapping and searching still use the original words. Testing showed it gets more than 9 in 10 words fully right in everyday Standard Arabic but does badly on dialects, so dialect speech is left plain unless you ask for it.

### Making sense of every language
- **Real words in Japanese and Chinese.** *These languages put no spaces between words, and the device's own word splitter cut 食べました ("ate") into four meaningless pieces.* Tamkeen carries a Japanese word analyser that keeps a verb together with its endings, and splits Chinese by finding the most likely chain of known words, after turning traditional characters into simplified ones so 學習 and 学习 count as the same word. Readings sit over the words: furigana over kanji only, and pinyin coloured by tone.
- **How Thai words are said.** *Thai learners need to see the tones, and many Thai words in real text are compounds no dictionary lists.* Tamkeen shows a romanised reading with tone marks over each word, and builds a compound's reading from the dictionary words that spell it exactly, using the fewest pieces plus rules that stop wrong splits. Checked against the compounds Wiktionary does list, it agrees 95% of the time.
- **One word, all its forms.** *A learner who knows "hablar" shouldn't see "hablaste" or "dámelo" as new words.* Tamkeen finds the dictionary form behind each word: in Spanish it peels off attached pronouns, using where the written accent falls to tell *cómetelo* ("eat it") from a lookalike verb, and it even reads Argentine forms like *tenés*; in German it reunites split verbs such as *fängt … an* into *anfangen* across the sentence and takes long compound words apart; in Russian it files a verb under its partner (сказать under говорить) and shows where the stress falls.
- **Words built from pieces.** *In Korean, Tagalog and Bisaya, the core of a word hides inside particles, prefixes, infixes and repeated syllables.* For Korean, Tamkeen splits each written syllable into its letters so it can strip particles and verb endings back to the dictionary form; its word list was ranked by reading 6.3 million subtitle words with the analyser itself, four rounds over. For Tagalog it tries removing prefixes, the -um- and -in- infixes and doubled syllables in every order, keeping only results that are real words (kumain, kinain and pagkain all become kain, "eat").
- **An Arabic analyser that fits in a phone.** *Arabic words carry prefixes, suffixes and attached pronouns, and the best university analyser is built to run only on computers.* Tamkeen rewrote its lookup tables to run on any device, then picks between possible readings using how common each is, how the word is spelled and the word before it, and switches on an Egyptian, Levantine or Gulf dictionary when it spots tell-tale dialect words. Words you have never tapped show in purple, tracked by dictionary form, so you can see at a glance what is new to you.
- **Bisaya, a language speech recognition didn't know.** *Whisper has no Bisaya (Cebuano), and cloud services fail on it.* At first Tamkeen asked Whisper for Tagalog with a Bisaya hint; now it downloads a version of Whisper retrained on Bisaya (5.9% of letters wrong instead of 15.4%) and converts it on your own computer with a small converter whose result is byte-for-byte what the official, much heavier tools produce. Meanings come partly from a public-domain Bisaya dictionary from the 1970s, with its older spellings folded so they match how Bisaya is written today.
- **Catching made-up words.** *Speech models sometimes keep "talking" after a recording goes quiet, inventing sentences nobody said.* Tamkeen spots these invented stretches because they pile up far faster than anyone can speak (more than 40 letters a second) and cuts them, and it drops the stock phrases models are known to invent over silence, such as "subscribe to the channel".

### Media from the big services, on their terms
- **YouTube, watched but never downloaded.** *Learners want clickable captions on YouTube videos, but YouTube doesn't allow downloading videos or drawing over its player.* Videos play in YouTube's own official embedded player, only the captions are fetched, and the clickable words sit below the video instead of on top of it. Tamkeen estimates the playback position between the player's own updates, so the highlighted word glides smoothly along.
- **Captions for videos that have none.** *Many videos have no captions in the language you're learning.* Tamkeen tries a person's captions, then YouTube's automatic ones, then YouTube's own translation; because YouTube hands its translations only to its own player, Tamkeen simply asks that player to show them over the picture. For a video with no captions at all, Google's Gemini can write them from the video's link alone, so nothing is ever downloaded.
- **A "trending" page YouTube no longer has.** *YouTube closed its Trending page in 2025, and a signed-out YouTube opens on an empty screen.* Tamkeen builds its own: a search for one very common word of the language, limited to this week's uploads and sorted by views, with country choices that add a dialect word (Egyptian "عشان", Moroccan "ديال") to bring up that dialect's own videos. Topic menus such as news, food and comedy use search words that were checked against YouTube's real results in every language.
- **Netflix with clickable subtitles.** *Netflix's video is copy-protected, so no other app can play it.* On the computer, Netflix's own player runs in a frameless window that Tamkeen places exactly over its player area and that never takes the keyboard; Tamkeen hides Netflix's controls, picks the soundtrack and subtitles in your language, and turns the subtitle file Netflix itself downloads into clickable words (in the web app, a browser extension does the same). Netflix's sound is never recorded (cards use a card voice instead), its subtitles are never shared or exported, and you can switch between, say, Standard Arabic and dialect subtitles in the middle of a show.
- **Reels: the words without the video.** *Short videos are great listening practice, but keeping copies of them isn't an option.* The computer listens to each reel once to write down its words (and, for reels it finds itself, checks they are really in your language with enough speech), then keeps only the reel's address and words; the phone plays it from Instagram's own page with the clickable words underneath. A rule for near ties stops a reel being thrown out just because the speech model wavered between two languages.
- **Popular podcasts in any language.** *There is no chart of "top podcasts in Arabic" or "in Persian".* Tamkeen reads Apple's free public charts for several countries where the language is spoken, keeps only the shows really in that language (judged by their script or common little words), and merges the lists. Apple has no Iran store, so Persian uses Afghanistan's and Tajikistan's charts; for Bisaya, which appears in no chart at all, a hand-researched list of the most-rated shows stands in.
- **Saying no when the rules say no.** *Some clever features would break a service's terms.* Spotify lyrics were removed as soon as it was clear they went against Spotify's terms; a Thai speech model was swapped for another (Pathumma) because its terms didn't allow sharing it in an app; and Audible files are turned away, since opening them would mean breaking their copy protection, with a pointer to shops that sell DRM-free audiobooks instead.

### One app everywhere, synced through your own Drive
- **One codebase, four apps.** *Building separate desktop, Android, iPhone and web apps would multiply the work and let them drift apart.* The screens are written once; on phones, a stand-in answers the same 70-odd requests the desktop's engine answers, straight from the phone's own storage, and the web app is that same phone build hosted free on GitHub Pages, with a free Cloudflare go-between fetching feeds and pages a web page can't fetch itself. An automatic test fails whenever one version is missing something another has.
- **Sync with no company server.** *Syncing between devices usually means a company's server holding your data.* Tamkeen keeps your library in a private Google Drive folder that only Tamkeen can read, and each device merges changes by when they were made, with deletion markers so something you removed stays removed everywhere. Small rules prevent accidents, such as a fresh, unplayed creature egg never overwriting a game that has progress.
- **Changes that arrive in seconds.** *Checking Drive often is slow and wasteful, so a review done on the computer could take ten minutes to reach the phone.* Each device makes one tiny check of whether the library file has changed since last time and stops there if not; after uploading, it sends a "look now" nudge on a private channel whose name is worked out from your own Drive file, so only your devices know it. The usual delay fell from 5–10 minutes to about 2–5 seconds.
- **Anki decks built on the phone.** *Making an Anki deck with audio normally needs desktop software.* The phone builds the whole deck itself: it cuts each word's audio clip out of the episode by copying sound packets without decoding them, writes Anki's database file from scratch and packs it up. Your review progress travels with it, turned into Anki's own schedule.
- **Two-way Anki sync that won't clobber your edits.** *Syncing with Anki risks one side overwriting the other's changes.* The front and back of each card are compared with what both sides held at the last sync, so the side that changed wins and only a true clash is settled by time; the image field, the review timer and your other decks are never touched. On Android it talks to AnkiDroid directly, and the computer and phone run identical rules, checked against dozens of shared test cases.
- **An interface in 17 languages without rewriting a page.** *Translating an app usually means changing every line of text in its code.* Tamkeen's screens are written in English, and a watcher swaps each piece of text for its translation the moment it appears on screen, including patterns like "12 words due". Anything that is study material is marked so it is never translated by mistake.
- **An iPhone app without a Mac.** *iPhone apps are normally built on a Mac and sold through the App Store.* Tamkeen's iPhone version is built automatically on GitHub's Macs every time the code changes and installed by sideloading with AltStore and a free Apple ID; its built-in phone features mirror the Android ones, call for call.

### Studying smarter
- **Flashcards that remember answers, not scores.** *Two devices each keeping their own card scores will disagree after a sync.* The review system (FSRS, a modern spaced-repetition model, with just two buttons) stores only the list of answers you gave and works out each card's state by replaying them. Combining two devices is then simply joining the two lists.
- **An honest "time left today".** *Review timers that guess from fixed numbers are often far off.* Tamkeen measures your own speed, separately for new words, reviews and second tries, and stops every clock while the app is hidden, so a card left open doesn't count as a slow answer. Tested on simulated learners over 90 days, its error dropped from 24% to 16%; and the ADHD-friendly card timer only turns a calmer colour when you go over, with no flashing or sounds.
- **Courses checked by ear.** *A course voice that mispronounces a word teaches the mistake.* Every language has a course of its 1,000 most common words (counted by dictionary form) with everyday sentences, voiced in advance; then every recording was played back to Whisper and scored letter by letter, with results of 98–100%. The free offline voice was rejected for courses after this same check caught it mispronouncing Arabic words.
- **Teaching a voice the Baghdad "g".** *Azure's Iraqi voices silently skip the letters گ and چ that Iraqi Arabic uses.* The Iraqi course spells those words out in phonetic symbols for the voice, which then says g and ch properly, and the build stops if any word with those letters isn't covered. For Moroccan Arabic, a sound check showed the voices read it like Standard Arabic, so that course has no audio rather than wrong audio.
- **Fixing what the transcript misheard.** *Speech recognition sometimes writes down a similar-sounding wrong word.* When you tap such a word, the AI says so (only when it is confident, and never for names, slang or dialect), the popup explains the word actually said, and one tap fixes the transcript on all your devices, with Undo.
- **Pronunciation practice that keeps nothing.** *Learners want to compare their voice with a native one without their recordings being stored.* "Say it" listens until you stop talking, trims the silence and turns a quiet recording up, then plays the card's sentence, a half-second pause and your attempt. Nothing is saved or sent anywhere.

### The creature game
- **Battles that are vocabulary reviews.** *Games can easily distract from studying.* Every move in a battle needs a right answer about one of your own saved words: the world freezes for the question with a few seconds on the clock, and a wrong or slow answer makes the move fail. The balance was tuned with the computer playing both sides in hundreds of trial battles, so at 80% right answers you win about four battles in five.
- **Fair arenas from a single number.** *Random maps can hand one side all the good ground, and sending whole maps between players is heavy.* Each arena grows from a single seed number, so two friends only share that number and get the same map; the ground is blended with its mirror image so both starting areas are identical, a walkable path between them is always opened, and an unfair map is redrawn. All 1,440 test maps passed these checks.
- **Terrain that isn't made of squares.** *Square tiles make every shore and field edge look blocky.* The ground is drawn on a second grid shifted half a tile, where each cell picks its picture by which kind of ground sits at each of its four corners (the "dual grid" trick), so edges curve and blend into each other, and textures and decorations vary across the map in natural-looking patches.
- **Ten gym leaders with brains of their own.** *Making rivals harder by just boosting their numbers feels unfair.* Each leader has its own dials for how often it thinks, how well it dodges, whether it aims where you are running, how much it cares about element matchups and good ground, and when it heals or interrupts; the time you get to answer also shrinks from 6 to 3.5 seconds. Every leader was tested in 150 simulated battles so that each one is clearly harder than the last.
- **A game controller for the whole app.** *Controllers behave differently on every device, and some gadgets pretend to be one.* Tamkeen reads controllers the same way on computer, phone and web, with its own button layout for each screen and a drawing of the controller in Settings that shows what every button does there. A device only counts as a controller once a real button is pressed, which stopped a set of computer speakers from showing up as one and an Xbox controller's resting trigger from scrolling the page by itself.

## Part 3 · Why it's designed this way: the research

Each design choice below says what Tamkeen does, what studies have found about it, and the studies themselves. Where the research is thin, mixed, or points the other way, it says so: some choices rest on strong evidence, and others are sensible guesses or simply there because they're fun.

### Reviews spaced out over time
**What Tamkeen does:** Tamkeen schedules every card with FSRS (the Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler, a memory model), aiming for a 90% chance that you still remember the word when it comes back. A word you know well comes back less and less often.
**What the research says:** Reviews spread out over time are remembered far better than the same number of reviews crammed together. This is one of the most reliable findings in memory research. A meta-analysis of 317 experiments found that spaced study beats massed study, and that the best gap between reviews grows the longer you need to remember (Cepeda et al., 2006). A meta-analysis of second-language studies found the same: spacing had a medium-to-large effect, and longer gaps beat shorter ones on delayed tests. In those studies, gaps that grow over time worked about as well as equal gaps (Kim & Webb, 2022). In a nine-year study of foreign-language vocabulary, 13 review sessions held 56 days apart gave about the same long-term memory as 26 sessions held 14 days apart (Bahrick et al., 1993; only four learners). FSRS builds on the work of Ye, Su and Cao (2022). They trained a memory model on 220 million review records from the MaiMemo language app and used it to schedule reviews at lower cost, reporting a 12.6% gain over earlier methods; the method was later extended (Su et al., 2023). Ebbinghaus (1885) first described the forgetting curve, and a 2015 single-learner replication produced a very similar curve. Limits: the FSRS papers measure how well the scheduler predicts memory and how few reviews it needs, using app data. They are not controlled tests of how much people learn. The 90% target is a trade-off between more reviews and more forgetting; no study fixes that number.

### Recall before you see the answer
**What Tamkeen does:** The front of a card shows the word and its sentence, and you try to remember the meaning before you turn it over. The answer is always shown after a miss.
**What the research says:** Trying to recall an answer strengthens memory more than reading it again. This is the "testing effect". In Roediger and Karpicke (2006), rereading did better on a test five minutes later, but practice testing gave much better memory days later. Karpicke and Roediger (2008) found the same with foreign-language word pairs: once a word had been answered correctly, testing it again greatly improved recall a week later, while studying it again did not. Students' predictions of how well they would do bore no relation to their actual results. A large meta-analysis confirmed the effect and found that recalling an answer helps more than recognising it from options (Rowland, 2014). Showing the correct answer after a miss matters a lot: in one word-learning study it increased final retention by 494%, while feedback after a correct answer made little difference (Pashler et al., 2005). Limits: most studies use university students in labs or classrooms, but the effect has been replicated many times, including for second-language words (Barcroft, 2007).

### Just two answer buttons
**What Tamkeen does:** You answer each card with Remembered or Forgot, instead of the four grades many flashcard apps use.
**What the research says:** There is no published study that compares two self-rating buttons with four for how much people learn. The evidence is indirect. Anki's official FSRS documentation says FSRS works fine if you only use "Again" and "Good", and "in some cases, FSRS may even be more accurate" that way. This is developer documentation, not a peer-reviewed study. Separately, research on self-grading shows that how honestly you grade yourself matters. Students who overrated their own recall while studying did worse on the final test, most likely because they stopped studying items they wrongly thought they knew (Dunlosky & Rawson, 2012). A simple, honest "did I get it?" is a reasonable design, but no study proves two buttons beat four.

### Which side of the card shows what
**What Tamkeen does:** The front shows the word in the language you're learning, with its sentence. The back shows the meaning and the sentence's translation in your own language. This trains understanding first: recognising a word when you hear or read it.
**What the research says:** The clearest finding is that you get best at the direction you practise. Steinel, Hulstijn and Steinel (2007) taught 20 English idioms to 129 Dutch students in one direction or the other. On a production test, learners who had practised producing the idiom (L1→L2) did much better. On a recognition test, learners who had practised recognising it (L2→L1) did better. Webb (2009) found that receptive learning (foreign word → meaning) produced larger gains in receptive knowledge of meaning. Productive learning (meaning → foreign word) produced larger gains in many other areas, including spelling and productive knowledge of meaning and grammar, so Webb suggested productive learning may be the better choice if only one is used. Griffin and Harley (1996) also found L1→L2 the more "versatile" order when learners need both to understand and to produce words. Recognising a word's meaning is the easiest kind of word knowledge and producing it from memory is the hardest. This order held at every frequency level in a test with 435 learners (Laufer & Goldstein, 2004). Producing words is harder still when they don't fit your own language's sound patterns (Ellis & Beaton, 1993). The harder L1→L2 direction can pay off: Schneider, Healy and Bourne (2002) found that learning in the harder English→French direction reduced forgetting over a week. Level matters too. Terai, Yamashita and Pasich (2021) found that lower-proficiency learners learned more with L2→L1 practice and higher-proficiency learners with L1→L2. They suggest this explains why earlier studies disagreed, though their study had only 28 learners. Most of these are short lab or classroom studies with paired words.
**Adding the opposite direction (meaning on the front, foreign word on the back):** the research supports it for speaking and writing. Productive practice builds the ability to produce words, which receptive practice alone does much less (Steinel et al., 2007; Webb, 2009). It also brings some receptive knowledge (Webb, 2009; Griffin & Harley, 1996), and its extra difficulty can reduce forgetting (Schneider et al., 2002). For beginners the receptive direction is easier and may work better (Terai et al., 2021), so a sensible design is to keep foreign-word-first cards as the default and add meaning-first cards once a word is known, or for learners who want to speak and write. That ordering is Tamkeen's own reading of these studies from these studies; no study has tested it.

### Memory tricks: a sound-alike word and a picture
**What Tamkeen does:** The Mnemonic maker finds words in your own language that sound like the new word, and links each to the meaning with a picture line (Spanish *pato*, "duck": "Picture a duck balanced on top of a POT"). Keywords that are also close in meaning come first, and your reviews keep testing the word itself.
**What the research says:** The keyword method links a foreign word to a familiar word that sounds like it, then joins that word to the meaning with a vivid image. In the classic study, learners using keywords scored 72% on Russian vocabulary against 46% for a control group (Atkinson & Raugh, 1975). Atkinson (1975) described Russian and Spanish experiments in which keywords beat rote repetition, and Pressley, Levin and Delaney (1982) reviewed the method and its uses. There is an important limit: in several studies keyword learners forgot faster, so the advantage shrank or even reversed after a delay (Wang, Thomas & Ouellette, 1992; Wang & Thomas, 1995). Partly because the benefits seemed limited to short retention periods, one major review rated the method "low utility" (Dunlosky et al., 2013). Pairing keywords with retrieval practice helps. Keyword-plus-retrieval beat keywords alone a week later, and recalling a word through its keyword worked better than recalling it without one (Miyatsu & McDaniel, 2019). With repeated recall, learners gradually stop needing the keyword, and those who switch to recalling the word directly remember it better (Dikmans et al., 2020). This supports combining keyword cards with spaced reviews, as Tamkeen does. On whether keywords related in meaning work better, the evidence is thin. In one study, keywords with a meaningful link were better memory cues than unrelated ones, but experienced language learners in the same study did better with plain repetition than with keywords at all (van Hell & Mahn, 1997). Another study found that keywords that are easy-to-picture nouns help, verb keywords hurt, and for recalling the foreign word the keyword must sound very close to it (Ellis & Beaton, 1993).

### Pictures with words
**What Tamkeen does:** Mnemonic keywords come with a picture, or a picture line to imagine, linking the sound to the meaning, and you still recall the word on the card.
**What the research says:** Dual coding theory holds that the mind stores words and images in two linked systems, so information stored both ways has two routes back into memory (Paivio, 1971, 1986; Clark & Paivio, 1991). In reading studies, words explained with a picture plus text were remembered better than words explained with text alone (Chun & Plass, 1996). A meta-analysis found that text-plus-picture glosses had a moderate effect on vocabulary learning and helped beginners most (Yun, 2011). There is an important limit: in one set of experiments, pictures were no better than translations, because learners became overconfident when they saw a picture. Once that overconfidence was removed, including through retrieval practice, words learned with pictures were remembered better (Carpenter & Olson, 2012). Pictures help most when paired with real recall, as on Tamkeen's cards.

### Every card has an example sentence
**What Tamkeen does:** Cards carry a real sentence using the word, with its translation on the back, so you see how the word is used.
**What the research says:** The research here is more mixed than people expect. An example sentence helps you understand a word in the moment, but studies find it adds little to remembering it. Measuring word knowledge in ten different ways, Webb (2007) found no significant difference between learning words as plain pairs and learning them from a single glossed sentence. Prince (1996) found that learning from translations led to more words remembered than learning from context, and that weaker learners had trouble using the words they had learned in new contexts. Words learned in lists or single sentences were remembered better than words met in a longer text (Laufer & Shmueli, 1997). The strongest result is that recall matters more than context: an informative sentence helped learners understand a word during practice, but practice that made them recall the meaning gave better memory later (van den Broek et al., 2018). Sentences on cards are useful for understanding and for showing real use (a reasonable aim, though not shown to add memory), while the recall step does most of the memory work.

### Sentences you can read
**What Tamkeen does:** When Tamkeen finds an example sentence for a new card, it prefers one where every other word is one you already know, so the new word is the only unknown.
**What the research says:** Krashen's input hypothesis says we acquire language by understanding input that is just a little beyond our current level ("i+1"). It is very influential, but it is a theory that is hard to test directly, and it is still debated. The research on lexical coverage is more concrete: you need to know nearly all the words in a text to understand it without help. In the best-known study, about 98% of the words (roughly one unknown word in 50) had to be known for most learners to understand a story adequately (Hu & Nation, 2000). Later work suggested 98% as the optimal level and 95% as a minimum (Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski, 2010). A study of 661 learners found no sharp threshold: comprehension rose steadily as more words were known (Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe, 2011). A 2023 replication could not fully reproduce the original 98% result (Kremmel et al., 2023), so treat the exact figure with caution. The general principle is solid, though: understanding drops quickly as unknown words pile up. Keeping each new sentence to one unknown word, with its meaning available, fits that principle.

### Learning from real podcasts, videos and books
**What Tamkeen does:** Instead of textbook dialogues, you learn from real podcasts, YouTube, Netflix, reels, audiobooks, books and web pages, with a transcript or captions in the language you're learning.
**What the research says:** Same-language captions help. A meta-analysis of 18 studies found a large effect of captions on both listening comprehension and vocabulary learning (Montero Perez, Van Den Noortgate & Desmet, 2013). Vanderplank's early study of 15 learners found that those above intermediate level got a great deal of usable input from subtitled TV and learned to use the subtitles flexibly, but it was of limited value for low-level learners (Vanderplank, 1988). He later wrote a book reviewing more than 30 years of research on captions (Vanderplank, 2016). Watching ordinary TV teaches new words incidentally, especially words that come up often (Peters & Webb, 2018). However, real TV needs about 3,000 of the most frequent word families, plus names, to understand 95% of the words, and about 7,000 to reach 98% (Webb & Rodgers, 2009). Authentic material therefore works best once learners know the most common words, or with help such as captions and tap-to-translate. Limits: most of the studies are short and measure gains right after viewing.

### Read along while you listen
**What Tamkeen does:** The players show the transcript as the audio plays and light up each word as it is said.
**What the research says:** Reading while listening helps. Over 13 weeks, learners who read graded readers while listening to the audio improved their listening fluency most consistently, compared with reading only or listening only (Chang & Millett, 2014). Audio-assisted reading also raised reading speed and comprehension more than silent reading for beginners (Chang & Millett, 2015). Repeated reading with audio support led to more vocabulary learning than repeated reading without it (Webb & Chang, 2012), and reading and listening to ten graded readers led to sizeable vocabulary gains that were largely kept three months later (Webb & Chang, 2015). Not every study is as positive: in one, learners could recall the meaning of only about 1 in 28 new words met while reading (with or without audio), and none met by listening alone (Brown, Waring & Donkaewbua, 2008). The word-by-word highlighting itself is less proven. In a study of children reading in their own language, highlighting each word as it was spoken changed their eye movements (fewer jumps back) but did not improve learning. The poorest readers liked it most (Gerbier, Bailly & Bosse, 2018). The read-along is well supported; the highlighting is a comfort feature without proven learning gains.

### Tap a word for its meaning
**What Tamkeen does:** Tap any word in a transcript, page, book or caption to see its dictionary form and meaning straight away, and save it as a card.
**What the research says:** Giving readers the meaning of unknown words while they read helps them both understand and learn those words. In a classic study, marginal glosses and dictionaries both helped students learn words that appeared more than once, compared with no help. Dictionary users often skipped the look-up or guessed wrongly, which weakened the benefit (Hulstijn, Hollander & Greidanus, 1996). A one-tap look-up removes most of that effort. A meta-analysis of computer glosses found a medium effect on reading comprehension and a large effect on incidental vocabulary learning (Abraham, 2008). Meanings given in the learner's own language were remembered better than meanings given in English (Laufer & Shmueli, 1997). Glosses with both text and a picture worked best for comprehension, and every type of gloss helped learners notice and recognise more target words than no gloss (Yanguas, 2009).

### The most common words first
**What Tamkeen does:** Each language's Basics course is its 1,000 most common words, with sentences and natural audio, and those words are the first to become cards.
**What the research says:** Learning words in order of frequency pays off because the commonest words come up again and again in everything you read and hear. Using frequency lists from a large corpus, Nation (2006) estimated that understanding written English without help (98% of words known) needs about 8,000–9,000 word families, and spoken English about 6,000–7,000. Schmitt and Schmitt (2014) argue that the top 3,000 word families should count as "high-frequency" vocabulary and be taught first. The first 1,000 words are the natural starting point on that path. Even TV needs about the top 3,000 families for 95% coverage (Webb & Rodgers, 2009). Limits: these figures come from English corpora. The principle carries over to other languages, but the exact numbers will differ.

### Grammar explained clearly
**What Tamkeen does:** The Grammar courses explain each point step by step, in the language you speak, with examples you can hear.
**What the research says:** Teaching grammar explicitly works. A meta-analysis of 49 studies found that focused instruction produced large gains that lasted, and that explicit instruction (explaining the rule) worked better than implicit instruction (Norris & Ortega, 2000). A later meta-analysis of 41 studies found explicit instruction beat implicit instruction for both simple and complex grammar points, and helped learners use the forms in spontaneous speech and writing as well as in controlled exercises (Spada & Tomita, 2010). Limits: Norris and Ortega warn that the kind of test used affects how large the effects look, and the studies define "explicit" in different ways. There is no solid research on whether grammar explanations work better in the learner's own language than in the target language, so Tamkeen makes no claim about the language of the explanations.

### A game around the learning
**What Tamkeen does:** Studying earns XP and coins for your creatures, which hatch from eggs and fight in live battles and gym battles where each move is a word question.
**What the research says:** Gamification has small but real benefits. A meta-analysis found significant effects on learning (g = 0.49), motivation (g = 0.36) and behaviour (g = 0.25). The learning effect held up in the best-designed studies; the motivation and behaviour effects were less stable (Sailer & Homner, 2020). A meta-analysis of serious games found they taught more than ordinary instruction and helped retention, but were not more motivating (Wouters et al., 2013). A review of gamified English-learning studies found positive experiences and outcomes, but no study could say which game elements caused the learning (Dehghanzadeh et al., 2021). Limits: results vary a lot between studies. Game features help most as a wrapper around proven methods such as recall and spacing, not as a replacement for them.

### Daily streaks
**What Tamkeen does:** Home shows your daily flashcard streak, and the game's goal streak can be protected with a freeze, so one missed day doesn't wipe out your progress.
**What the research says:** Doing something daily in the same situation gradually makes it automatic. In a 12-week study, 96 volunteers repeated a daily behaviour at the same point in their day. How automatic it felt rose along a curve that levelled off after anywhere from 18 to 254 days, depending on the person. Missing a single day did not materially affect the process (Lally et al., 2010). On streaks specifically, seven studies found that showing people an unbroken streak made them more likely to keep going than showing them a broken one. Losing a streak hurt more when people blamed themselves, and less when they could "repair" it (Silverman & Barasch, 2023). Streaks motivate, but a broken streak can make people quit, so a forgiving design (a missed day isn't fatal, or a broken streak can be repaired) fits the research. That design point is Tamkeen's reading from these two studies.
**Studies:**
- Lally, P., van Jaarsveld, C. H. M., Potts, H. W. W., & Wardle, J. (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. *European Journal of Social Psychology, 40*(6), 998–1009. [https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)

### Marking words you know
**What Tamkeen does:** Words you've met are coloured as you read: amber means not known yet. Hold a word to mark it known, so you notice what you still need to learn.
**What the research says:** Schmidt's "noticing hypothesis" argues that language input only becomes learning when you consciously notice it, and that paying attention to what is to be learned helps (Schmidt, 1990). Marking a word as known or not yet known makes you stop and notice it. This is a theoretical argument, and it is debated. Ticking the words you know is also a recognised way to measure vocabulary: the yes/no test format (Meara & Buxton, 1987) does exactly this, and adds made-up words to catch people who overclaim. Limits: there is no study testing whether marking words while reading improves learning, so this feature rests on theory rather than direct evidence. Self-marking can also be over-generous (see the section on two answer buttons).

### Studying with friends
**What Tamkeen does:** Friends can see each other's streaks and what they are studying, chat, join guilds and challenge each other's creatures.
**What the research says:** Tracking your progress helps you reach goals, and it helps more when others can see it. A meta-analysis of 138 experiments (nearly 20,000 people) found that prompting people to monitor their progress improved goal attainment (d = 0.40). The effect was larger when progress was reported to others or made public, and when it was physically recorded (Harkin et al., 2016). In gamified learning, combining competition with collaboration was especially effective for changing behaviour (Sailer & Homner, 2020), and educational games produced larger learning gains when people played in groups (Wouters et al., 2013). Limits: none of this research tests friends in a language app specifically. The evidence is about goal tracking and group play in general.

### Hearing every word
**What Tamkeen does:** Cards play the audio they were saved from when it can be kept, and otherwise a natural voice reads the word and the sentence aloud.
**What the research says:** Hearing words helps you learn their sound, and hearing them in several voices helps more. When English speakers learned Spanish words, hearing each word from several speakers led to better learning than hearing it from one (Barcroft & Sommers, 2005). Seeing the written word while hearing it improved later recognition of the words compared with sound or text alone (Bird & Williams, 2002). You learn the form you are exposed to: learners who saw captions got better at recognising words in writing, while those who heard the audio got better at recognising them by ear (Sydorenko, 2010). Memory for speech sounds is thought to be a key tool for learning new words (Baddeley, Gathercole & Papagno, 1998). Limits: these are mostly short lab studies. There is no study comparing recorded native speakers with synthetic voices.

### Quick questions in the battle game
**What Tamkeen does:** In live battles you answer a word question within a few seconds to land a move. The review cards themselves are never timed out: their time bar is only a guide.
**What the research says:** There is no study testing a short answer timer like this one. Bjork's "desirable difficulties" idea says some things that make practice feel harder, such as spacing and testing yourself, improve long-term memory (Bjork, 1994; Bjork & Bjork, 2011). Effortful recall that still succeeds is better for memory than easy recall (Pyc & Rawson, 2009). But the difficulty has to be one the learner can overcome; otherwise it is simply undesirable (Bjork & Bjork, 2011). Pressure can backfire: when quizzes were framed as high-stakes, they lost their advantage over rereading, while low-stakes quizzes kept it (Hinze & Rapp, 2014). Taken together, the timer is best treated as a fun game mechanic, not a learning booster, and the main reviews should stay untimed. That recommendation is Tamkeen's own reading of these studies.

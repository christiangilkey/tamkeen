# What makes Tamkeen special

Tamkeen (تمكين, "empowerment" in Arabic) has its own solutions to many problems that language learners and language apps run into. Each one below gives the problem in *italics*, then how Tamkeen solves it.

### Free and private by design
- **Speech recognition on your own computer.** *Paid transcription services charge by the hour and send your audio away.* Tamkeen runs Whisper, a free speech-recognition model, on your own computer, and it writes down every word with the exact moment it is said, so the player can light up each word as you hear it. If your computer is too slow, you can use a cloud service with your own key instead: Tamkeen first finds the speech, cuts it at pauses and sends only that, so you never pay for silence or music (about nine hours of audio for a dollar).
- **A dictionary first, AI only when it's needed.** *Explaining words with AI needs a paid key that many learners don't have.* Every language has a large built-in dictionary, made from Wiktionary plus scholarly Arabic and Bisaya dictionaries, that downloads once and then works offline. By default the dictionary answers first and the AI is asked only about words it lacks; a small tag on every word popup says where the meaning came from, and a tap on it switches the source.
- **Memory tricks found by sound, with no AI.** *The "keyword method" needs a word in your own language that sounds like the foreign one, and AI often suggests words that don't sound alike at all.* Tamkeen breaks both words into speech sounds using a pronunciation dictionary and measures how close they really are, giving extra weight to the stressed syllable, so only true sound-alikes get through (Spanish *pato*, "duck", gives "pot"). On 38 words with published keyword examples, all 39 good keywords passed and all 42 bad ones failed; a map of related meanings then puts keywords that also hint at the meaning first (German *Hund* gives "hound").
- **Example sentences you can actually read.** *A sentence full of unknown words teaches very little.* For each card, Tamkeen searches a downloaded collection of real sentences with translations and prefers one where every other word is one you have already met, counting every form of a word as the same word. For a learner who knows 1,500 common words, the share of readable sentences rose from about a third to four in five.
- **A voice for every card, three ways.** *Cards saved from YouTube, Netflix, web pages or books have no sound you are allowed to keep.* Each card can be read by your device's own free voice, by Microsoft's natural-sounding Azure voices on your own free account (called straight from your device, with nothing in between), or by a free voice program on your computer whose recordings sync to your phone. Voices with non-commercial licences were left out, and the word and its sentence are joined into one small sound file without being re-encoded.
- **A word list from a photo.** *Typing up a vocabulary list from a textbook is slow.* Photograph the page and Tamkeen reads it, either with your AI or entirely on the device with an open-source text reader that downloads once (about 6 MB for Spanish and English). It rebuilds the page's columns into "word – meaning" pairs, drops headings and page numbers, flags shaky readings for you to check, and never keeps the photo (on Android it even deletes the copy the camera app leaves behind).
- **Vowel marks for Arabic.** *Arabic is usually written without its short vowels, which makes reading aloud hard for learners.* A small open model on your computer adds them to whole transcripts in about 3 seconds per hour of audio, and they sync to your phone; only what is drawn on screen changes, so tapping and searching still use the original words. Testing showed it gets more than 9 in 10 words fully right in everyday Standard Arabic but does badly on dialects, so dialect speech is left plain unless you ask for it.

### Making sense of every language
- **Real words in Japanese and Chinese.** *These languages put no spaces between words, and the device's own word splitter cut 食べました ("ate") into four meaningless pieces.* Tamkeen carries a Japanese word analyser that keeps a verb together with its endings, and splits Chinese by finding the most likely chain of known words, after turning traditional characters into simplified ones so 學習 and 学习 count as the same word. Readings sit over the words: furigana over kanji only, and pinyin coloured by tone.
- **How Thai words are said.** *Thai learners need to see the tones, and many Thai words in real text are compounds no dictionary lists.* Tamkeen shows a romanised reading with tone marks over each word, and builds a compound's reading from the dictionary words that spell it exactly, using the fewest pieces plus rules that stop wrong splits. Checked against the compounds Wiktionary does list, it agrees 95% of the time.
- **One word, all its forms.** *A learner who knows "hablar" shouldn't see "hablaste" or "dámelo" as new words.* Tamkeen finds the dictionary form behind each word: in Spanish it peels off attached pronouns, using where the written accent falls to tell *cómetelo* ("eat it") from a lookalike verb, and it even reads Argentine forms like *tenés*; in German it reunites split verbs such as *fängt … an* into *anfangen* across the sentence and takes long compound words apart; in Russian it files a verb under its partner (сказать under говорить) and shows where the stress falls.
- **Words built from pieces.** *In Korean, Tagalog and Bisaya, the core of a word hides inside particles, prefixes, infixes and repeated syllables.* For Korean, Tamkeen splits each written syllable into its letters so it can strip particles and verb endings back to the dictionary form; its word list was ranked by reading 6.3 million subtitle words with the analyser itself, four rounds over. For Tagalog it tries removing prefixes, the -um- and -in- infixes and doubled syllables in every order, keeping only results that are real words (kumain, kinain and pagkain all become kain, "eat").
- **An Arabic analyser that fits in a phone.** *Arabic words carry prefixes, suffixes and attached pronouns, and the best university analyser is built to run only on computers.* Tamkeen rewrote its lookup tables to run on any device, then picks between possible readings using how common each is, how the word is spelled and the word before it, and switches on an Egyptian, Levantine or Gulf dictionary when it spots tell-tale dialect words. Words you have never tapped show in purple, tracked by dictionary form, so you can see at a glance what is new to you.
- **Bisaya, a language speech recognition didn't know.** *Whisper has no Bisaya (Cebuano), and cloud services fail on it.* At first Tamkeen asked Whisper for Tagalog with a Bisaya hint; now it downloads a version of Whisper retrained on Bisaya (5.9% of letters wrong instead of 15.4%) and converts it on your own computer with a small converter whose result is byte-for-byte what the official, much heavier tools produce. Meanings come partly from a public-domain Bisaya dictionary from the 1970s, with its older spellings folded so they match how Bisaya is written today.
- **Catching made-up words.** *Speech models sometimes keep "talking" after a recording goes quiet, inventing sentences nobody said.* Tamkeen spots these invented stretches because they pile up far faster than anyone can speak (more than 40 letters a second) and cuts them, and it drops the stock phrases models are known to invent over silence, such as "subscribe to the channel".

### Media from the big services, on their terms
- **YouTube, watched but never downloaded.** *Learners want clickable captions on YouTube videos, but YouTube doesn't allow downloading videos or drawing over its player.* Videos play in YouTube's own official embedded player, only the captions are fetched, and the clickable words sit below the video instead of on top of it. Tamkeen estimates the playback position between the player's own updates, so the highlighted word glides smoothly along.
- **Captions for videos that have none.** *Many videos have no captions in the language you're learning.* Tamkeen tries a person's captions, then YouTube's automatic ones, then YouTube's own translation; because YouTube hands its translations only to its own player, Tamkeen simply asks that player to show them over the picture. For a video with no captions at all, Google's Gemini can write them from the video's link alone, so nothing is ever downloaded.
- **A "trending" page YouTube no longer has.** *YouTube closed its Trending page in 2025, and a signed-out YouTube opens on an empty screen.* Tamkeen builds its own: a search for one very common word of the language, limited to this week's uploads and sorted by views, with country choices that add a dialect word (Egyptian "عشان", Moroccan "ديال") to bring up that dialect's own videos. Topic menus such as news, food and comedy use search words that were checked against YouTube's real results in every language.
- **Netflix with clickable subtitles.** *Netflix's video is copy-protected, so no other app can play it.* On the computer, Netflix's own player runs in a frameless window that Tamkeen places exactly over its player area and that never takes the keyboard; Tamkeen hides Netflix's controls, picks the soundtrack and subtitles in your language, and turns the subtitle file Netflix itself downloads into clickable words (in the web app, a browser extension does the same). Netflix's sound is never recorded (cards use a card voice instead), its subtitles are never shared or exported, and you can switch between, say, Standard Arabic and dialect subtitles in the middle of a show.
- **Reels: the words without the video.** *Short videos are great listening practice, but keeping copies of them isn't an option.* The computer listens to each reel once to write down its words (and, for reels it finds itself, checks they are really in your language with enough speech), then keeps only the reel's address and words; the phone plays it from Instagram's own page with the clickable words underneath. A rule for near ties stops a reel being thrown out just because the speech model wavered between two languages.
- **Popular podcasts in any language.** *There is no chart of "top podcasts in Arabic" or "in Persian".* Tamkeen reads Apple's free public charts for several countries where the language is spoken, keeps only the shows really in that language (judged by their script or common little words), and merges the lists. Apple has no Iran store, so Persian uses Afghanistan's and Tajikistan's charts; for Bisaya, which appears in no chart at all, a hand-researched list of the most-rated shows stands in.
- **Saying no when the rules say no.** *Some clever features would break a service's terms.* Spotify lyrics were removed as soon as it was clear they went against Spotify's terms; a Thai speech model was swapped for another (Pathumma) because its terms didn't allow sharing it in an app; and Audible files are turned away, since opening them would mean breaking their copy protection, with a pointer to shops that sell DRM-free audiobooks instead.

### One app everywhere, synced through your own Drive
- **One codebase, four apps.** *Building separate desktop, Android, iPhone and web apps would multiply the work and let them drift apart.* The screens are written once; on phones, a stand-in answers the same 70-odd requests the desktop's engine answers, straight from the phone's own storage, and the web app is that same phone build hosted free on GitHub Pages, with a free Cloudflare go-between fetching feeds and pages a web page can't fetch itself. An automatic test fails whenever one version is missing something another has.
- **Sync with no company server.** *Syncing between devices usually means a company's server holding your data.* Tamkeen keeps your library in a private Google Drive folder that only Tamkeen can read, and each device merges changes by when they were made, with deletion markers so something you removed stays removed everywhere. Small rules prevent accidents, such as a fresh, unplayed creature egg never overwriting a game that has progress.
- **Changes that arrive in seconds.** *Checking Drive often is slow and wasteful, so a review done on the computer could take ten minutes to reach the phone.* Each device makes one tiny check of whether the library file has changed since last time and stops there if not; after uploading, it sends a "look now" nudge on a private channel whose name is worked out from your own Drive file, so only your devices know it. The usual delay fell from 5–10 minutes to about 2–5 seconds.
- **Anki decks built on the phone.** *Making an Anki deck with audio normally needs desktop software.* The phone builds the whole deck itself: it cuts each word's audio clip out of the episode by copying sound packets without decoding them, writes Anki's database file from scratch and packs it up. Your review progress travels with it, turned into Anki's own schedule.
- **Two-way Anki sync that won't clobber your edits.** *Syncing with Anki risks one side overwriting the other's changes.* The front and back of each card are compared with what both sides held at the last sync, so the side that changed wins and only a true clash is settled by time; the image field, the review timer and your other decks are never touched. On Android it talks to AnkiDroid directly, and the computer and phone run identical rules, checked against dozens of shared test cases.
- **An interface in 17 languages without rewriting a page.** *Translating an app usually means changing every line of text in its code.* Tamkeen's screens are written in English, and a watcher swaps each piece of text for its translation the moment it appears on screen, including patterns like "12 words due". Anything that is study material is marked so it is never translated by mistake.
- **An iPhone app without a Mac.** *iPhone apps are normally built on a Mac and sold through the App Store.* Tamkeen's iPhone version is built automatically on GitHub's Macs every time the code changes and installed by sideloading with AltStore and a free Apple ID; its built-in phone features mirror the Android ones, call for call.

### Studying smarter
- **Flashcards that remember answers, not scores.** *Two devices each keeping their own card scores will disagree after a sync.* The review system (FSRS, a modern spaced-repetition model, with just two buttons) stores only the list of answers you gave and works out each card's state by replaying them. Combining two devices is then simply joining the two lists.
- **An honest "time left today".** *Review timers that guess from fixed numbers are often far off.* Tamkeen measures your own speed, separately for new words, reviews and second tries, and stops every clock while the app is hidden, so a card left open doesn't count as a slow answer. Tested on simulated learners over 90 days, its error dropped from 24% to 16%; and the ADHD-friendly card timer only turns a calmer colour when you go over, with no flashing or sounds.
- **Courses checked by ear.** *A course voice that mispronounces a word teaches the mistake.* Every language has a course of its 1,000 most common words (counted by dictionary form) with everyday sentences, voiced in advance; then every recording was played back to Whisper and scored letter by letter, with results of 98–100%. The free offline voice was rejected for courses after this same check caught it mispronouncing Arabic words.
- **Teaching a voice the Baghdad "g".** *Azure's Iraqi voices silently skip the letters گ and چ that Iraqi Arabic uses.* The Iraqi course spells those words out in phonetic symbols for the voice, which then says g and ch properly, and the build stops if any word with those letters isn't covered. For Moroccan Arabic, a sound check showed the voices read it like Standard Arabic, so that course has no audio rather than wrong audio.
- **Fixing what the transcript misheard.** *Speech recognition sometimes writes down a similar-sounding wrong word.* When you tap such a word, the AI says so (only when it is confident, and never for names, slang or dialect), the popup explains the word actually said, and one tap fixes the transcript on all your devices, with Undo.
- **Pronunciation practice that keeps nothing.** *Learners want to compare their voice with a native one without their recordings being stored.* "Say it" listens until you stop talking, trims the silence and turns a quiet recording up, then plays the card's sentence, a half-second pause and your attempt. Nothing is saved or sent anywhere.

### The creature game
- **Battles that are vocabulary reviews.** *Games can easily distract from studying.* Every move in a battle needs a right answer about one of your own saved words: the world freezes for the question with a few seconds on the clock, and a wrong or slow answer makes the move fail. The balance was tuned with the computer playing both sides in hundreds of trial battles, so at 80% right answers you win about four battles in five.
- **Fair arenas from a single number.** *Random maps can hand one side all the good ground, and sending whole maps between players is heavy.* Each arena grows from a single seed number, so two friends only share that number and get the same map; the ground is blended with its mirror image so both starting areas are identical, a walkable path between them is always opened, and an unfair map is redrawn. All 1,440 test maps passed these checks.
- **Terrain that isn't made of squares.** *Square tiles make every shore and field edge look blocky.* The ground is drawn on a second grid shifted half a tile, where each cell picks its picture by which kind of ground sits at each of its four corners (the "dual grid" trick), so edges curve and blend into each other, and textures and decorations vary across the map in natural-looking patches.
- **Ten gym leaders with brains of their own.** *Making rivals harder by just boosting their numbers feels unfair.* Each leader has its own dials for how often it thinks, how well it dodges, whether it aims where you are running, how much it cares about element matchups and good ground, and when it heals or interrupts; the time you get to answer also shrinks from 6 to 3.5 seconds. Every leader was tested in 150 simulated battles so that each one is clearly harder than the last.
- **A game controller for the whole app.** *Controllers behave differently on every device, and some gadgets pretend to be one.* Tamkeen reads controllers the same way on computer, phone and web, with its own button layout for each screen and a drawing of the controller in Settings that shows what every button does there. A device only counts as a controller once a real button is pressed, which stopped a set of computer speakers from showing up as one and an Xbox controller's resting trigger from scrolling the page by itself.

# What makes Tamkeen special

Tamkeen (تمكين, "empowerment" in Arabic) has its own solutions to many problems that language learners and language apps run into. Each one below gives the problem in *italics*, then how Tamkeen solves it.

### Learning from what you already love
- **Your shows are the lessons.** *Textbook dialogues are written to teach a word list, not to be enjoyed, and few learners keep going for long with material they don't care about.* In Tamkeen the study material is whatever you already want to hear, watch or read: the podcasts you follow, YouTube, Netflix, reels, audiobooks, books and web pages, each with a transcript or captions where every word can be tapped. You save a word straight from the line it was said in, so learning happens inside things you would have watched or read anyway.
- **Real language, made readable.** *Material made for native speakers shows how a language is really spoken (its speed, its everyday phrases, its slang and dialects), but on its own it is too hard for most learners.* Tamkeen doesn't simplify the material; it adds help around it. Tap any word for its meaning, read along as each word lights up while it is said, switch on a translation, see at a glance in purple which words are new to you, and add the vowel marks Arabic usually leaves out. The Basics courses teach each language's 1,000 most common words first, so real material becomes readable sooner.
- **Staying with what you like.** *Hopping between unrelated material means meeting new words all the time.* Follow a podcast or a YouTube channel and its new episodes and videos are kept at hand. The same speakers and topics bring the same words back again and again, so each episode is a little easier than the last, and the words you saved keep turning up in context. (The research behind this is on the Supporting studies page.)
- **A free shelf of classics, ready to read.** *Free classics online are scattered and messy: a long book on Wikisource is split over dozens of pages, its poems are set out in tables, and the text comes mixed with navigation links, editors' notes, page numbers and the odd stray edit.* Tamkeen's own shelf builder fetches each book from Project Gutenberg, Wikisource or Aozora Bunko, follows its contents pages chapter by chapter, keeps the headings, paragraphs and lines of verse, and strips everything else, with fixes made book by book where the general cleaning can't see a problem. Only works that are public domain both where they were written and in the United States are chosen, and a book too long for one volume comes as two instead of being cut short. The shelf holds 217 books in 18 languages; the Arabic shelf has 21, among them the Quran, the six books of hadith (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim in two volumes each, al-Nasa'i's as far as it has been typed), the first books of al-Kafi, the Mu'allaqat, the Thousand and One Nights and Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddimah.

### Free and private by design
- **Speech recognition on your own computer.** *Paid transcription services charge by the hour and send your audio away.* Tamkeen runs Whisper, a free speech-recognition model, on your own computer, and it writes down every word with the exact moment it is said, so the player can light up each word as you hear it. If your computer is too slow, you can use a cloud service with your own key instead: Tamkeen first finds the speech, cuts it at pauses and sends only that, so you never pay for silence or music (about nine hours of audio for a dollar).
- **A dictionary first, AI only when it's needed.** *Explaining words with AI needs a paid key that many learners don't have.* Every language has a large built-in dictionary, made from Wiktionary plus scholarly Arabic and Bisaya dictionaries, that downloads once and then works offline. By default the dictionary answers first and the AI is asked only about words it lacks; a small tag on every word popup says where the meaning came from, and a tap on it switches the source.
- **Memory tricks found by sound, with no AI.** *The "keyword method" needs a word in your own language that sounds like the foreign one, and AI often suggests words that don't sound alike at all.* Tamkeen breaks the new word into speech sounds (from Wiktionary's pronunciation, or the language's spelling rules), compares them with the sounds of every word in an English pronunciation dictionary and measures how close they really are, giving extra weight to the stressed syllable, so only true sound-alikes get through (Spanish *pato*, "duck", gives "pot"). On 38 words with published keyword examples, all 39 good keywords passed and all 42 bad ones failed; a map of related meanings then puts keywords that also hint at the meaning first (German *Hund* gives "hound").
- **Sound-alikes for the word itself, not its little extras.** *A word is often saved with pieces that aren't worth memorising by sound: Arabic's ال ("the"), German's der/die/das, a "to" before an English verb, Tagalog's mga, a Japanese する.* Before it looks for sound-alikes, the Mnemonic maker leaves these out in every language Tamkeen teaches, so الكتاب is matched as "kitaab", not "al-kitaab", and the card says what was left out. It knows the exceptions, too: words where ال really belongs to the word (الآن, "now"; اليوم, "today"), and words that only look as if they start with it (ألف, "thousand").
- **A Mnemonic maker that learns from your own tricks.** *A free sound-alike finder can't know which kinds of pictures work for you.* Write your own memory trick with the sound-alike word in CAPITALS ("a duck sitting on a POT") and Tamkeen checks that it really sounds like the word, learns the kinds of keywords you like, and can offer your own words again for other words that sound like them. Everything else you write is ignored, and with AI, a few of your own tricks are shown to it as examples of your style.
- **Example sentences you can actually read.** *A sentence full of unknown words teaches very little.* For each card, Tamkeen searches a downloaded collection of real sentences with translations and prefers one where every other word is one you have already met, counting every form of a word as the same word. For a learner who knows 1,500 common words, the share of readable sentences rose from about a third to four in five.
- **A sentence for every common word, with no AI.** *A collection of real sentences may never use a word in exactly the form a card has it: "problem" in Arabic is مشكلة, but sentences say المشكلة, مشكلتي or مشاكل, and a German card says "der Teil" where sentences say "ein Teil" or "Teile".* Each sentence file now carries a small table of which dictionary word every word in it is a form of (plurals, broken plurals, verb forms, Arabic pronoun endings, worked out from Wiktionary), Tamkeen looks for the word without the article or notes typed round it, and each Basics course's own sentence is there as a last resort. Words of the 1,000 most common with a real Tatoeba or Wiktionary sentence rose from 921 to 997 in German and from 881 to 985 in Korean, and every one of the 1,000 now gets a sentence in all 16 course languages.
- **A voice for every card, three ways.** *Cards saved from YouTube, Netflix, web pages or books have no sound you are allowed to keep.* Each card can be read by your device's own free voice, by Microsoft's natural-sounding Azure voices on your own free account (called straight from your device, with nothing in between), or by a free voice program on your computer whose recordings sync to your phone. Voices with non-commercial licences were left out, and the word and its sentence are joined into one small sound file without being re-encoded.
- **A word list from a photo.** *Typing up a vocabulary list from a textbook is slow.* Photograph the page and Tamkeen reads it, either with your AI or entirely on the device with an open-source text reader that downloads once (about 6 MB for Spanish and English). It rebuilds the page's columns into "word – meaning" pairs, drops headings and page numbers, flags shaky readings for you to check, and never keeps the photo (on Android it even deletes the copy the camera app leaves behind).
- **Vowel marks for Arabic.** *Arabic is usually written without its short vowels, which makes reading aloud hard for learners.* A small open model on your computer adds them to whole transcripts in about 3 seconds per hour of audio, and they sync to your phone; only what is drawn on screen changes, so tapping and searching still use the original words. Testing showed it gets more than 9 in 10 words fully right in everyday Standard Arabic but does badly on dialects, so dialect speech is left plain unless you ask for it.

### Your data, kept safe
- **Your library stays yours.** *Most learning apps keep your progress, notes and recordings on the company's servers.* Your podcasts, transcripts, saved words, settings and keys are kept on your own device. Sync puts a copy in a hidden folder of your own Google Drive that only Tamkeen can open, so Tamkeen's developer has no copy and no way in. Friends, chat and guilds are the only parts that use an online database, and only if you turn them on.
- **Keys that go only where they belong.** *A key typed into an app can leak if other programs or websites can ask for it.* Your AI and voice keys are sent only to the service they belong to, and to your own Drive when you sync. On the computer, Tamkeen's Google sign-in and its link to Anki are never handed to any page at all, not even Tamkeen's own; the other keys go only to Tamkeen's own page, and only with the secret pass described next.
- **A locked door on the computer.** *A desktop app that runs a small web server on your computer can be reached by any website you visit, including by tricks that make a website pose as your own computer.* Tamkeen's server answers only requests addressed to itself from its own page; anything that changes your data needs a secret pass that is made fresh each time the app starts and is kept only in memory; and strict browser rules let only Tamkeen's own code (and YouTube's official player) run in its window. A backup you restore is cleaned of any sign-ins, keys and odd file paths before it replaces your library.
- **Online features from 16, without keeping your birthday.** *Apps that check age often store your full date of birth.* Friends, chat and guilds open at 16. Tamkeen asks your month and year of birth once and keeps only the month you turn 16, never the date itself. Under 16, the online features stay off and the device is signed out of them.
- **Nothing a friend sends can run on your device.** *Anything that arrives from another player could be altered on the way.* A friend's creatures and game details are checked again when they arrive and are shown only as plain text and pictures, never as page code, so a tampered profile can't do anything on your device.
- **A go-between that keeps nothing.** *A web page can't fetch other websites for itself, so a web app needs a server's help, and that server sees what passes through it.* The web app's go-between server, which fetches podcast feeds, web pages and captions for your browser, passes on no cookies and stores nothing itself. AI requests never go through it: your browser sends them straight to your AI provider.

### Making sense of every language
- **Real words in Japanese and Chinese.** *These languages put no spaces between words, and the device's own word splitter cut 食べました ("ate") into four meaningless pieces.* Tamkeen carries a Japanese word analyser that keeps a verb together with its endings, and splits Chinese by finding the most likely chain of known words, after turning traditional characters into simplified ones so 學習 and 学习 count as the same word. Readings sit over the words: furigana over kanji only, and pinyin coloured by tone.
- **How Thai words are said.** *Thai learners need to see the tones, and many Thai words in real text are compounds no dictionary lists.* Tamkeen shows a romanised reading with tone marks over each word, and builds a compound's reading from the dictionary words that spell it exactly, using the fewest pieces plus rules that stop wrong splits. Checked against the compounds Wiktionary does list, it agrees 95% of the time.
- **One word, all its forms.** *A learner who knows "hablar" shouldn't see "hablaste" or "dámelo" as new words.* Tamkeen finds the dictionary form behind each word: in Spanish it peels off attached pronouns, using where the written accent falls to tell *cómetelo* ("eat it") from a lookalike verb, and it even reads Argentine forms like *tenés*; in German it reunites split verbs such as *fängt … an* into *anfangen* across the sentence and takes long compound words apart; in Russian it files a verb under its partner (сказать under говорить) and shows where the stress falls.
- **Grammar at a glance, in every language.** *A dictionary that gives only a meaning leaves out what a learner needs to use the word: is it masculine or feminine, what is its plural, how does the verb change?* Tamkeen reads the line at the top of each Wiktionary entry, where these facts are kept, for all 18 languages it teaches, and shows them on the word popup: the part of speech and gender, an Arabic word's broken plurals and a verb's present tense and verbal noun, a German noun's genitive and plural, a Latin verb's principal parts, a Russian verb's partner in the other aspect, Thai and Korean counter words, and the present and imperfect subjunctive of Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Catalan and French verbs (taken from the full conjugation table, since the top line leaves them out). Forms anyone could guess (casa, casas; walk, walked) are left out so the irregular ones stand out, and Chinese measure words, which Wiktionary writes inside the meaning, get a line of their own. About 690,000 dictionary entries carry these details, and the dictionaries already on your devices pick them up by themselves.
- **Arabic roots, recovered.** *Arabic words are organised by their root (ك ت ب gives كتاب "book", كاتب "writer" and مكتب "office"), but Wiktionary's downloadable data had moved the root to a hidden category, and its full download keeps it for only some words, so the roots had gone missing from the dictionary altogether.* Tamkeen reads the root wherever it now sits, then fills the gaps from a scholarly Arabic dictionary (CAMeL), matching words by their letters and doubled letters rather than their vowel marks, which the two dictionaries write differently, and only when the match is certain. Within one spelling it shares a root only among words of the same dialect, after a check caught a Moroccan word for "loquats" picking up the root of "joking". Now 19,715 of 26,555 Standard Arabic entries show their root, and Persian words borrowed from Arabic show the Arabic root too, traced through the Arabic word Wiktionary says they came from.
- **Words built from pieces.** *In Korean, Tagalog and Bisaya, the core of a word hides inside particles, prefixes, infixes and repeated syllables.* For Korean, Tamkeen splits each written syllable into its letters so it can strip particles and verb endings back to the dictionary form; its word list was ranked by reading 6.3 million subtitle words with the analyser itself, four rounds over. For Tagalog it tries removing prefixes, the -um- and -in- infixes and doubled syllables in every order, keeping only results that are real words (kumain and kinain both become kain, "eat").
- **An Arabic analyser that fits in a phone.** *Arabic words carry prefixes, suffixes and attached pronouns, and the best university analyser is built to run only on computers.* Tamkeen rewrote its lookup tables to run on any device, then picks between possible readings using how common each is, how the word is spelled and the word before it, and switches on an Egyptian, Levantine or Gulf dictionary when it spots tell-tale dialect words. Words you have never tapped show in purple, tracked by dictionary form, so you can see at a glance what is new to you.
- **Bisaya, a language speech recognition didn't know.** *Whisper has no Bisaya (Cebuano), and cloud services fail on it.* At first Tamkeen asked Whisper for Tagalog with a Bisaya hint; now it downloads a version of Whisper retrained on Bisaya (5.9% of letters wrong instead of 15.4%) and converts it on your own computer with a small converter whose result is byte-for-byte what the official, much heavier tools produce. Meanings come partly from a public-domain Bisaya dictionary from the 1970s, with its older spellings folded so they match how Bisaya is written today.
- **Catching made-up words.** *Speech models sometimes keep "talking" after a recording goes quiet, inventing sentences nobody said.* With the retrained models that do this most (such as Thai's), Tamkeen spots invented stretches because they pile up far faster than anyone can speak (more than 40 letters a second, where real speech runs at 10 to 20) and cuts them, and keeps a phrase said over and over only once; for Thai this halved the wrong characters, from 17.5% to 8.7%. In cloud transcripts it drops the stock phrases models are known to invent over silence, such as "subscribe to the channel". The computer's own Whisper avoids the problem by transcribing only the stretches where it has found speech.

### Our own word parsers
Besides the analysers above, Tamkeen has its own parsers for every language it teaches. Each one takes a word as it is written in real text and works out what the dictionary calls it and how it sounds.
- **Hindi read the way it is said.** *Hindi spelling shows an "a" sound that speakers often drop (कमरा is said "kamrā", not "kamarā"), and many words can be spelled two ways.* Tamkeen works out where the unsaid "a" falls by counting back from the end of the word, and folds the two spellings of a word into one. Its readings match Wiktionary's for 98.8% of the 1,000 most common words and about 90% of the 30,000 most common, and for the rest it carries Wiktionary's own reading.
- **Mending Thai word breaks.** *The phone's built-in Thai word breaker cuts some common words in two (โรงพยาบาล, "hospital", into "building" and "nurse") and puts a letter on the wrong side of spoken particles such as เนี่ย.* Tamkeen rejoins neighbouring pieces that together spell one of the 1,000 most common words (except a short list that are usually two words) and moves the stray letter back. Measured against Thai split by hand, the device's breaker does as well as a leading Thai splitter (89% against 88% of word boundaries right), and 97% of the pieces of spoken Thai are real words.
- **Bisaya roots.** *In Bisaya an ending can shuffle a word's sounds (inom, "drink", becomes imnon), so cutting the ending off doesn't give the root.* Tamkeen undoes those swaps, counts a verb prefix only when the root also appears as a verb in at least two forms (so borrowed words like "mobile" stay whole), and keeps a list of words that only look built. The share of words in five books that get a Bisaya meaning rose from 78.9% to 83.4%.
- **Bisaya verbs from a dictionary's code.** *Wiktionary lists few Bisaya verb forms, and a Bisaya verb changes shape for who does the action, what it is done to and whether it has happened.* Wolff's 1972 Bisaya dictionary marks each verb with a short code (such as [A; c1]) saying which of these forms it takes. Tamkeen decodes those codes for 12,191 verbs and shows the main forms on the word popup, future then past (kaon, "eat": mokaon · mikaon, kan-on · gikaon). Each form is spelled the way Wolff's own examples or today's Bisaya writing spell it, and a form it can't confirm is left out rather than guessed.
- **Tagalog verbs, focus by focus.** *A Tagalog verb changes with its focus (who or what the sentence is about) and its aspect, so kumain, kinain, kakainin and kainan are all forms of kain, "eat".* Wiktionary's conjugation tables arrive flattened into one long list; Tamkeen sorts the forms back into place by their shape (finding 95% of the aspect forms the entries' top lines name) and shows a word's root, the affix that built it (-um-) and the verb's other forms. A form with no entry of its own, such as sinulat, says which form it is and leads to its verb. Hand-checked roots from a Tagalog treebank (UD Tagalog-NewsCrawl) were used to test and extend the root finder, which now agrees with them for 81% of verbs from Wiktionary alone, up from 76%.
- **Latin as it is really printed.** *Latin is printed with and without long-vowel marks, with j or i and v or u, and it hangs "and" (-que) and a question mark (-ne) onto words.* Tamkeen folds all those spellings into one, takes off -que, -ne and -ve, and looks the word up among nearly a million word forms. 89.5% of the 1.44 million words in 38 classic books are found, and about 250 hand fixes catch cases where the obvious reading is wrong (*est* is "is", not "eats").
- **Persian verbs and half spaces.** *Persian writes some words with an invisible "half space" that many people don't type, and builds each verb from a prefix, a stem and a person ending.* Tamkeen treats می‌خواهم and میخواهم as one word, takes verbs apart into prefix, one of 667 stems and ending, reads a stem with an ending but no prefix as a noun (کارم, "my work"), and rejoins the pieces speech recognition splits apart.
- **French, Italian, Portuguese and Catalan.** *These languages glue words together: l'homme, dell'anno, dimmi ("tell me"), and in Portuguese a pronoun can sit inside the verb (dir-lhe-ei, "I will tell him").* Tamkeen takes them apart to find the dictionary word: *dimmi* gives *dire*, *fazê-lo* gives *fazer*.
- **English phrasal verbs.** *"Gave it up" doesn't mean "gave".* Tamkeen undoes contractions and recognises about 330 phrasal verbs as one word even with a word in between, so "gave it up" is looked up as "give up".
- **Korean as it sounds.** *Korean letters change sound next to each other (같이 is said "gachi").* Tamkeen writes Korean in Latin letters with all those sound-change rules worked out, so nothing has to be downloaded.
- **How any word sounds.** *Not every word has a recorded pronunciation.* Tamkeen has spelling-to-sound rules for 15 writing systems, cuts the sounds into syllables and puts the stress where each language puts it (Spanish on the second-last syllable unless the word ends in a consonant other than n or s). The Mnemonic maker uses them when Wiktionary has no pronunciation. For Bisaya, where spelling doesn't show the stress (palit is said pa-LIT, hatag HA-tag), it uses the stress marks in Wolff's dictionary first.

### Our own file readers
- **Podcast feeds the phone can read.** *A single stray character makes a phone's built-in reader reject a whole podcast feed.* The phone has its own forgiving feed reader that recovers from broken tags. On 7 real feeds with 5,790 episodes it found exactly the same episodes, titles, audio, dates and pictures as the well-known reader the computer uses.
- **Books in every common format, opened on the device.** *Ebooks come in many formats, and most apps need large add-ons or a server to open them.* Tamkeen reads EPUB, Word (.docx and the old .doc), Kindle MOBI and AZW3, and PDF itself, with its own code for zip files, Word's old file structure and Kindle's compression (PDFs are drawn with Mozilla's PDF.js); books with copy protection are recognised and turned away. In a PDF it rebuilds paragraphs and headings from the gaps and text sizes on the page and removes page numbers and end-of-line hyphens.
- **A book's language, read from all of it.** *Many free ebooks open with an English notice and a contents list of "Page 1, Page 2…", so an Arabic book from the Internet Archive was taken for English and filed out of sight.* Tamkeen reads samples from all through a book, skipping short lines like page numbers, and uses the language label inside an EPUB to settle what the letters alone can't.
- **Chapters found in about 20 languages.** *Plain text has no marked headings.* Tamkeen spots chapter headings from the words for "chapter" in about 20 languages (第…章, 제…장, บทที่, الفصل), turns a book's opening lines into one title, and doesn't mistake a play's capitalised speaker names for headings. It was checked on about 200 books.
- **The article, not the clutter.** *Web pages wrap a story in menus, adverts and links.* Tamkeen keeps the part of the page with the most paragraph text, skips parts named like page furniture, drops lists that are only links, and keeps bold and italic. The same code runs in the computer's and Android's built-in browsers.
- **Netflix subtitles.** *Netflix's subtitle files use a format that the browser extension can't normally read.* Tamkeen has its own reader for them that understands all their ways of writing times, removes sound labels and speaker dashes, refuses oversized or booby-trapped files, and prefers the full subtitles over the "forced" ones that only translate signs. It is checked by 38 tests on the computer and in the extension.
- **YouTube captions, word by word.** *Automatic captions time each word, but hand-made ones only time each line.* Tamkeen keeps the automatic captions' word timings, spreads a hand-made line's time across its words by their length, and never lets times run backwards, so the highlighted word moves smoothly.
- **Cutting audio without decoding it.** *Cloud transcription services take only small files.* Tamkeen cuts an MP3 into pieces of at most 8 minutes or 10 MB by reading the file's frame headers, without decoding the sound, so every piece starts at its exact time.
- **Putting cloud transcripts back together.** *Cloud services return the words and the sentences separately.* Tamkeen puts each word into the sentence its middle falls in, and keeps the sentence's own punctuation when the word counts agree.
- **Fixing a speech model's line breaks.** *The retrained Bisaya speech model starts lines without a space, which glued words onto the line before and shifted later words one line early (13 of 68 lines in a test).* Tamkeen holds lines back until their letters match their words again, then shares the words out and splits a glued word's time by its letters.
- **Pasting a word list.** *Word lists come with tabs, semicolons, equals signs or dashes between the word and its meaning.* Tamkeen accepts all of them, doesn't split at a hyphen inside a word, and turns round a line written meaning-first by recognising which side is in the language you're learning.

### Media from the big services, on their terms
- **YouTube, watched but never downloaded.** *Learners want clickable captions on YouTube videos, but YouTube doesn't allow downloading videos or drawing over its player.* Videos play in YouTube's own official embedded player, only the captions are fetched, and the clickable words sit below the video instead of on top of it. Tamkeen estimates the playback position between the player's own updates, so the highlighted word glides smoothly along.
- **Captions for videos that have none.** *Many videos have no captions in the language you're learning.* Tamkeen tries a person's captions, then YouTube's automatic ones, then YouTube's own translation; because YouTube hands its translations only to its own player, Tamkeen simply asks that player to show them over the picture. For a video with no captions at all, Google's Gemini can write them from the video's link alone, so nothing is ever downloaded. Lists of videos say so before you tap: a video with no captions carries a small "No captions · Gemini required" tag, found by asking YouTube's player only about the videos on screen, three at a time.
- **Dubbed versions, flagged before you tap.** *YouTube now offers some videos dubbed into other languages, but a list of videos doesn't say so, and a dub isn't the video's own sound.* Tamkeen reads the same short answer it already asks YouTube for each video in a list (the one that says whether it has captions) and tags a video whose own sound is another language but which YouTube also offers in yours: **Arabic track available**. It costs no extra question to YouTube and never touches YouTube's player.
- **A "trending" page YouTube no longer has.** *YouTube closed its Trending page in 2025, and a signed-out YouTube opens on an empty screen.* Tamkeen builds its own: a search for one very common word of the language, limited to this week's uploads and sorted by views, with country choices that add a dialect word (Egyptian "عشان", Moroccan "ديال") to bring up that dialect's own videos. Topic menus such as news, food and comedy use search words that were checked against YouTube's real results in every language.
- **Netflix with clickable subtitles.** *Netflix's video is copy-protected, so no other app can play it.* On the computer, Netflix's own player runs in a frameless window that Tamkeen places exactly over its player area and that never takes the keyboard; Tamkeen hides Netflix's controls, picks the soundtrack and subtitles in your language, and turns the subtitle file Netflix itself downloads into clickable words (in the web app, a browser extension does the same). Netflix's sound is never recorded (cards use a card voice instead), its subtitles are never shared or exported, and you can switch between, say, Standard Arabic and dialect subtitles in the middle of a show.
- **Reels: the words without the video.** *Short videos are great listening practice, but keeping copies of them isn't an option.* The computer listens to each reel once to write down its words (and, for reels it finds itself, checks they are really in your language with enough speech), then keeps only the reel's address and words; the phone plays it from Instagram's own page with the clickable words underneath. A rule for near ties stops a reel being thrown out just because the speech model wavered between two languages.
- **Popular podcasts in any language.** *There is no chart of "top podcasts in Arabic" or "in Persian".* Tamkeen reads Apple's free public charts for several countries where the language is spoken, keeps only the shows really in that language (judged by their script or common little words), and merges the lists. Apple has no Iran store, so Persian uses Afghanistan's and Tajikistan's charts; for Bisaya, which appears in no chart at all, a hand-researched list of the most-rated shows stands in.
- **Saying no when the rules say no.** *Some clever features would break a service's terms.* Spotify lyrics were removed as soon as it was clear they went against Spotify's terms; a Thai speech model was swapped for another (Pathumma) because its terms didn't allow sharing it in an app; and Audible files are turned away, since opening them would mean breaking their copy protection, with a pointer to shops that sell DRM-free audiobooks instead.

### One app everywhere, synced through your own Drive
- **One codebase, four apps.** *Building separate desktop, Android, iPhone and web apps would multiply the work and let them drift apart.* The screens are written once; on phones, a stand-in answers the same 70-odd requests the desktop's engine answers, straight from the phone's own storage, and the web app is that same phone build hosted free on GitHub Pages, with a free Cloudflare go-between fetching feeds and pages a web page can't fetch itself. An automatic test fails whenever one version is missing something another has.
- **Sync with no company server.** *Syncing between devices usually means a company's server holding your data.* Tamkeen keeps your library in a private Google Drive folder that only Tamkeen can read, and each device merges changes by when they were made, with deletion markers so something you removed stays removed everywhere. Small rules prevent accidents, such as a fresh, unplayed creature egg never overwriting a game that has progress.
- **Changes that arrive in seconds.** *Checking Drive often is slow and wasteful, so a review done on the computer could take ten minutes to reach the phone.* Each device makes one tiny check of whether the library file has changed since last time and stops there if not; after uploading, it sends a "look now" nudge on a private channel whose name is worked out from your own Drive file, so only your devices know it. The usual delay fell from 5–10 minutes to about 2–5 seconds.
- **Anki decks built on the phone.** *Making an Anki deck with audio normally needs desktop software.* The phone builds the whole deck itself: it cuts each word's audio clip out of the episode by copying sound packets without decoding them, writes Anki's database file from scratch and packs it up. Your review progress travels with it, turned into Anki's own schedule.
- **Two-way Anki sync that won't clobber your edits.** *Syncing with Anki risks one side overwriting the other's changes.* The front and back of each card are compared with what both sides held at the last sync, so the side that changed wins and only a true clash is settled by time; the image field, the review timer and your other decks are never touched. On Android it talks to AnkiDroid directly, and the computer and phone run identical rules, checked against 51 shared test cases.
- **An interface in 17 languages without rewriting a page.** *Translating an app usually means changing every line of text in its code.* Tamkeen's screens are written in English, and a watcher swaps each piece of text for its translation the moment it appears on screen, including patterns like "12 words due". Anything that is study material is marked so it is never translated by mistake.
- **An iPhone app without a Mac.** *iPhone apps are normally built on a Mac and sold through the App Store.* Tamkeen's iPhone version is built automatically on GitHub's Macs every time the code changes and installed by sideloading with AltStore and a free Apple ID; its built-in phone features mirror the Android ones, call for call.

### Studying smarter
- **Flashcards that remember answers, not scores.** *Two devices each keeping their own card scores will disagree after a sync.* The review system (FSRS, a modern spaced-repetition model, with just two buttons) stores only the list of answers you gave and works out each card's state by replaying them. Combining two devices is then simply joining the two lists.
- **An honest "time left today".** *Review timers that guess from fixed numbers are often far off.* Tamkeen measures your own speed, separately for new words, reviews and second tries, and stops every clock while the app is hidden, so a card left open doesn't count as a slow answer. Tested on simulated learners over 90 days, its error dropped from 24% to 16%; and the ADHD-friendly card timer only turns a calmer colour when you go over, with no flashing or sounds.
- **Courses checked by ear.** *A course voice that mispronounces a word teaches the mistake.* Every language has a course of its 1,000 most common words (counted by dictionary form) with everyday sentences, voiced in advance; then every recording was played back to Whisper and scored letter by letter, with results of 98–100%. The free offline voice was rejected for courses after this same check caught it mispronouncing Arabic words.
- **Teaching a voice the Baghdad "g".** *Azure's Iraqi voices silently skip the letters گ and چ that Iraqi Arabic uses.* The Iraqi course spells those words out in phonetic symbols for the voice, which then says g and ch properly, and the build stops if any word with those letters isn't covered. For Moroccan Arabic, a sound check showed the voices read it like Standard Arabic, so that course has no audio rather than wrong audio.
- **Each dialect word in its own accent.** *A Saudi voice reading an Iraqi or Egyptian word says it like Standard Arabic, and one "my dialect" setting can't serve someone learning two dialects.* With Azure, every Arabic card is read by a voice from the dialect the word comes from: Levantine by a Syrian voice, Egyptian by an Egyptian one, Iraqi by an Iraqi one, Gulf by a Saudi one, so Iraqi and Levantine cards sit side by side, each in its own accent; a grammar lesson's examples are read in its dialect too. Iraqi words with گ and چ that aren't in the course get their phonetic spelling worked out from their vowel marks, so the voice still says g and ch.
- **Fixing what the transcript misheard.** *Speech recognition sometimes writes down a similar-sounding wrong word.* When you tap such a word, the AI says so (only when it is confident, and never for names, slang or dialect), the popup explains the word actually said, and one tap fixes the transcript on all your devices, with Undo.
- **Pronunciation practice that keeps nothing.** *Learners want to compare their voice with a native one without their recordings being stored.* "Say it" listens until you stop talking, trims the silence and turns a quiet recording up, then plays the card's sentence, a half-second pause and your attempt. Nothing is saved or sent anywhere.
- **Studying for a test that has no glossary.** *DLI's ICPTs test reading and listening on real news, ads and broadcasts, and unlike unit tests they come with no word list.* DLI Arabic students get a 2,120-word ICPT deck by topic and level, built on how the test is scored: a level counts at about 70% of its questions, and only once the levels below it are passed. So the deck teaches every topic's Level 1 words before Level 2, while each topic's folders let a student work on a weak area. A word the student already has from the basic course is that same card, filed in a second folder, so nothing is learned twice.

### The creature game
- **Battles that are vocabulary reviews.** *Games can easily distract from studying.* Every move in a battle needs a right answer about one of your own saved words: the world freezes for the question with a few seconds on the clock, and a wrong or slow answer makes the move fail. The balance was tuned with the computer playing both sides in hundreds of trial battles, so at 80% right answers you win about four battles in five.
- **Battles on the words you need.** *Battles that always ask your newest words keep drilling what you just learned while older words wait.* Battle → Words → **Due for review** asks the words your flashcards have due, and **A folder** asks one folder's words, such as the chapter you have a test on. Either needs 20 words with a meaning; until then battles ask your newest words and a line under the choice says so. In these battles every creature fights at the same level, so a win comes from the words, not from how long a creature has been raised.
- **Battles that pay for learning, not grinding.** *If battles pay well, people fight for XP instead of learning words.* A battle's XP comes from the words it makes well known: 25 XP for each, plus 3 for a win or 1 for a loss. A word becomes well known only after four right answers spread over at least a week (the wait doubles: 1, 2, then 4 days), so fighting more battles in a day can't earn more of it.
- **Fair arenas from a single number.** *Random maps can hand one side all the good ground, and sending whole maps between players is heavy.* Each arena grows from a single seed number, so two friends only share that number and get the same map; the ground is blended with its mirror image so both starting areas are identical, a walkable path between them is always opened, and an unfair map is redrawn. All 1,440 test maps passed these checks.
- **Terrain that isn't made of squares.** *Square tiles make every shore and field edge look blocky.* The ground is drawn on a second grid shifted half a tile, where each cell picks its picture by which kind of ground sits at each of its four corners (the "dual grid" trick), so edges curve and blend into each other, and textures and decorations vary across the map in natural-looking patches.
- **Arenas with buildings you can walk through.** *A map that is only open ground plays the same every time, and buildings dropped onto a random map can wall a creature in or hand one side the only way through.* The village, dungeon and fortress maps place houses, rooms, corridors, towers and a keep as real walls and floors, then cut the doors and gates in both halves of the map alike, so the two starts are mirror images and there is always a way from one creature to the other. Walls block attacks too, so a corridor is a place to hide. 480 test maps of the three kinds passed every fairness check, each built in about 6 ms.
- **Roofs that get out of the way.** *Buildings seen from above either show their roofs, and hide whoever is inside, or have none and look unfinished.* Every house, shop, tavern, Town Hall and tower on a battle map has a roof, and it fades away while any creature, yours or your rival's, is under it, then comes back when they leave. So the map looks built, and neither side can hide in a building.
- **Secret rooms that stay fair.** *A hidden room with a reward is fun to find, but only one side finding it would decide the battle.* A dungeon hides rooms behind cracked walls, drawn as solid rock until the player clicks or taps the crack and the creature breaks it open. They always come in pairs, one on each side at the mirror-image spot, with the same spring and treasure inside, and the map's way-finding learns the new way through the moment the wall falls.
- **Friend battles on the same words.** *A battle between friends asks each player their own words, so one may get easy words and the other hard ones.* A folder challenge sends one folder's words to a friend who studies the same language, through the chat you already share, and you each battle the other's team with exactly those words; each result comes back with the folder's name. Nothing new is stored online: the words travel the way a shared folder always could, only between friends.
- **Colourings you earn by really doing it.** *A reward for "finishing a book" or "watching an episode" is easy to cheat by jumping to the end.* Seven special colourings each go to the creature that was out while you did something: saving 200 words, listening for 10 hours, reviewing 1,000 cards, reading a whole book, watching 10 Netflix episodes, beating a guild raid boss, or listening to 10 podcast episodes. A book page only counts once it has been on screen long enough to read (at most 400 words a minute), and a book only once its last page and 80% of it have been read that way; an episode only once playback reaches its end and 80% of it really played, checked in 5-second pieces at any speed, so seeking ahead or tapping skip never adds up. Each colouring is drawn differently, not just recoloured: glowing runes, a neon sunset that ripples to a beat, a foil card's shine, cut paper, an old TV's glitches, magma cracks with embers, or echoes of itself, and its moving parts cost only a few dots per frame, so battles stay smooth.
- **Shirts that fit any body.** *A striped shirt only makes sense on a creature that stands upright; on a four-legged one the stripes run from nose to tail.* Every shirt is an all-over pattern that reads the same whichever way a body lies (checks, prints scattered at all angles, a woven stripe, a zigzag lattice), and a test checks each pattern's same-colour runs are about as long across as down.
- **Clothes that follow the light.** *A pattern pasted onto pixel art looks flat, like a sticker.* A shirt's pattern takes on the light and shadow of the creature's body underneath, each pixel as much lighter or darker as the body was there, so stripes and argyle wrap round the creature instead of lying on top of it.
- **Animated without redrawing.** *A walk, an idle and a flight for 18 creatures in two looks each means hundreds of drawings, and turning pixel art like a photo smears its outlines.* Tamkeen makes all 396 frames from the finished drawings: every pixel belongs to a part (legs, wings, tail, head), and each frame swings, turns or bends the parts. The picture is enlarged 8 times with a pixel-art scaler and every pixel of the frame is fetched from where it came, so edges stay crisp, and whatever doesn't move stays exactly as drawn. The same pass works out, for every frame, the eyelids for blinking, where a shirt's pattern sits, and where hats and held things go, so clothes move with the body.
- **Ten gym leaders with brains of their own.** *Making rivals harder by just boosting their numbers feels unfair.* Each leader has its own dials for how often it thinks, how well it dodges, whether it aims where you are running, how much it cares about element matchups and good ground, and when it heals or interrupts; the time you get to answer also shrinks from 6 to 3.5 seconds. Every leader was tested in 150 simulated battles so that each one is clearly harder than the last.
- **A game controller for the whole app.** *Controllers behave differently on every device, and some gadgets pretend to be one.* Tamkeen reads controllers the same way on computer, phone and web, with its own button layout for each screen and a drawing of the controller in Settings that shows what every button does there. A device only counts as a controller once a real button is pressed, which stopped a set of computer speakers from showing up as one and an Xbox controller's resting trigger from scrolling the page by itself.

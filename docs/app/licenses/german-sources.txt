German word table and common-word list: today's spelling and capitals, from Wiktionary (optional download)

People learning German can download a table of German word forms and their dictionary forms
(Häuser -> Haus, fängt -> fangen, musste -> müssen), so every form of a word counts as the same word.
The table is not part of this program; the app downloads it only when the learner agrees
(docs/lexicon/de-lemmas.json.gz, made by tools/build_lemmas.py).

It is made from:

  Lemmatization Lists by Michal Měchura, https://github.com/michmech/lemmatization-lists
  (lemmatization-de.txt), available under the Open Database License (ODbL) 1.0:
  https://opendatacommons.org/licenses/odbl/1-0/

  wordfreq by Robyn Speer, https://github.com/rspeer/wordfreq (CC BY-SA 4.0): its word counts
  decide which forms are kept and in which order the dictionary forms are listed.

  The English Wiktionary's German entries (https://en.wiktionary.org, by the Wiktionary contributors),
  as extracted by Wiktextract (Tatu Ylonen, https://kaikki.org), CC BY-SA 4.0: which spellings the
  1996 spelling reform replaced (mußte -> musste, Fluß -> Fluss) and which are Switzerland's (Strasse),
  the words that never change form which the list leaves out (aber, dass, sehr), and how often each
  word is written with a capital letter in the middle of a sentence in the entries' example sentences
  (Zeit "time" is written so far more than the preposition zeit). Only these facts are taken, not
  Wiktionary's text.

The list of the 1000 commonest German words (web/js/wordlists/de.js, part of the app) is wordfreq's
ranking (CC BY-SA 4.0, see wordfreq.txt), with each word written as German writes it (Zeit, Straße,
weiß) using the same word list and Wiktionary facts.

The table is a derived database and is distributed under the Open Database License 1.0; the facts
taken from Wiktionary keep their CC BY-SA 4.0 licence. The files, the full licence texts and how to
rebuild them: https://github.com/christiangilkey/tamkeen/tree/main/docs/lexicon
